:::lede
GRC designs and runs the controls. Internal audit independently tests whether
they work. Security engineering builds the systems both are describing. The
three overlap in vocabulary and almost never in accountability.
:::

The confusion is worth resolving early, because the roles look identical from
outside - all three read system documentation, all three ask engineers
questions, all three write findings - and the difference is entirely about who
they answer to.

## The distinction that actually matters

:::versus a="GRC" a-badge="SECOND LINE" b="Internal audit" b-badge="THIRD LINE"
Designs the control, writes the policy, keeps the register, gathers the
evidence, answers the customer questionnaire. Accountable to management.
+++
Samples the same controls without having designed them, forms an opinion,
reports to the audit committee. Independence is the whole job.
:::

:::attrs a="GRC" b="Internal audit"
Reports to :: Management :: Audit committee or board
Owns the control :: +Yes :: -Never
Can fix what it finds :: +Yes, that is the job :: -No, independence forbids it
Main output :: Register, policy, evidence :: Audit report and findings
Typical entry role :: GRC analyst :: Internal audit associate
Certification path :: ISO 27001 LI, CRISC :: CIA, CISA
Moves easily to :: Risk, privacy, TPRM :: External audit, assurance
:::

Internal audit's constraint is the important one. An auditor who helps rewrite
the control they will later test has compromised the opinion they exist to give.
That is why an internal auditor will point at a gap and decline to close it - not
obstruction, but the only position from which the finding is worth anything.

GRC has the opposite mandate. Finding a gap and leaving it open is the failure.

## Where practitioners actually get confused

Three-quarters of the confusion is caused by one thing: in organisations under
roughly 500 people, the same person often does both. The company has a
"compliance manager" who writes the policy, runs the access review, and then
signs the attestation saying the access review happened.

That is a self-review, and every framework names it. It is workable while the
organisation is small and honest about it; it stops being workable the moment an
external party is relying on the attestation.

:::def term="Self-review threat" ref="1.5"
The impairment created when the party assessing a control is the party that
designed or operated it. Managed by separating the roles, not by declaring
independence.
:::

## The security engineering boundary

Security engineering builds and operates the control. GRC establishes that the
control is required, defines what "working" means, tests that it ran, and keeps
the proof.

In practice the boundary is drawn at the console. If the work involves changing a
configuration, it is engineering. If it involves deciding the configuration must
be a certain way, or demonstrating to a third party that it was, it is GRC.

The productive relationship between the two is narrower than either expects: GRC
should be able to state precisely what evidence it needs and why an obligation
demands it, and should not be specifying how the system produces it.

## The short version

Ask who receives the output. Management receives GRC's. The audit committee
receives internal audit's. If the answer is "the same person who asked for the
work", it is not audit - whatever the job title says.

:::lede
Risk quantification expresses exposure as a **monetary range - a probable loss
and its spread - instead of a colour on a heat map**. It answers the one
question a red, amber or green cell cannot: how much.
:::

## Qualitative and quantitative are not a hierarchy

Neither method outranks the other; each answers a different question.
Qualitative scoring - the likelihood-times-impact heat map at
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/) - triages breadth cheaply, ranking a whole
register in an afternoon. Quantification costs more, and earns its place on the
few decisions where a number actually changes the call: a large risk acceptance,
a control-spend trade-off or a board question about whether exposure sits inside
capacity ([3.4](/risk/risk-capacity/)).

The mistake is to treat one as the grown-up version of the other. A register
quantified end to end is not more mature than a well-kept heat map. It is usually
just more expensive.

## What a quantified risk looks like

A quantified risk is a loss, and a loss has two parts: how often the event
happens and how much it costs when it does. Loss equals frequency times
magnitude. Expressed honestly, each side is a range, so the output is a
distribution - a most-likely figure with a spread around it - not a single
number dressed up as certainty.

Money is the usual unit because it lets unlike risks be compared and weighed
against the cost of treating them. A count of records exposed or hours lost is
quantitative too; the financial form is simply the one that travels to a board.

The simplest form drops the spread and multiplies two point estimates.
Annualised loss expectancy (ALE) is single loss expectancy times the annual rate
of occurrence: a £2m loss expected once every ten years is an ALE of £200,000. It
is a starting point, not the destination.

FAIR - Factor Analysis of Information Risk, standardised by The Open Group as
Open FAIR - is the common open method for decomposing frequency and magnitude
into parts that can each be estimated. It gives a shared vocabulary and a
defensible structure. It does not manufacture data: the output is only ever as
sound as the estimates fed into it.

:::def term="Annualised loss expectancy" ref="3.1"
The expected cost of a risk over one year: single loss expectancy multiplied by
the annual rate of occurrence. A single figure, useful as a first cut rather than
the whole answer.
:::

## Where quantification goes wrong

**False precision.** A probability invented in a meeting does not become data
because it carries a decimal point. A confident number built on guesses is more
dangerous than an honest colour, because it travels further before anyone thinks
to question it.

**Quantifying everything.** The effort is justified by the decision it informs.
Running a full analysis on every line of the register spends the budget that the
three decisions actually needing it should have had.

**A model no one can explain.** If the board that has to trust the number cannot
follow how it was reached, the number carries no authority. Complexity that
cannot be explained to the person signing is a cost, not a feature.

:::faq
### What is the difference between qualitative and quantitative risk analysis?
Qualitative analysis ranks risks on a descriptive scale - usually likelihood
times impact rendered as a heat map - and is quick enough to run across an entire
register. Quantitative analysis expresses a risk as a monetary loss, or a range
of losses, and takes real effort to do well.

One sorts the population; the other sizes an individual decision. Most programmes
need both, applied where each fits.

### What is the FAIR model?
FAIR, Factor Analysis of Information Risk, is an open framework for quantifying
risk in financial terms. It breaks loss down into loss frequency and loss
magnitude, then decomposes each further so the parts can be estimated and
recombined. The Open Group maintains it as Open FAIR. It is a way to structure
the analysis, not a source of the underlying numbers.

### Do you need to quantify risk?
Not for every risk. Quantify where a number changes a decision - a large
acceptance, a spend trade-off, a capacity question
([3.4](/risk/risk-capacity/)) - and leave the rest on the qualitative heat map.
Quantifying everything wastes the effort that the decisions genuinely needing it
deserve.
:::

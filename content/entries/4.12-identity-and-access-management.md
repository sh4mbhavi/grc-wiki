:::lede
Identity and access management is the set of controls that decides **who holds an
account, what that account is allowed to reach, and how both are proven**, from
the moment a person joins to the moment their access is removed.
:::

It is the control domain auditors probe first, because it produces the clearest
evidence of whether the organisation does what its policies say. An access list
is a fact: either the leaver still has a login or they do not, either the
finance system enforces segregation of duties or it does not. There is little
room for a narrative.

Two ideas run under everything in the domain. **Least privilege**: an identity
gets the minimum access its role needs and no more. **Accountability**: every
action traces to one person, which is why shared and generic accounts are a
standing finding.

## The joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle

Most IAM failures are timing failures in this one process.

:::terms
Joiner :: Access is provisioned from an approved role template, granted on the start date and not before, with the approval recorded. Over-provisioning here ("give them what the last person had") is how entitlement creep starts.
Mover :: On a role change, old access is removed as new access is added. The step almost always skipped is the removal, so people accumulate rights as they move around and end up with a set nobody would approve from scratch.
Leaver :: Access is revoked on the last working day (ideally by disabling the identity centrally so every connected system follows), and privileged credentials are rotated. Delay here is the highest-severity gap in the domain.
:::

:::def term="Segregation of duties" ref="4.2"
A control ensuring no single person can both initiate and approve the same
sensitive transaction: raise a payment and release it, write code and deploy it
to production. Enforced through role design and tested by looking for identities
that hold both sides.
:::

## The access review

The recurring control that catches what the lifecycle missed. On a set cadence,
quarterly for privileged and financially significant systems and at least
annually for the rest, each system's access list goes to the person accountable
for that system, who confirms every entry is still needed or flags it for removal.

A review is only evidence if three things are true: the reviewer is the business
owner and not the IT team that grants the access, the flagged removals are
actually actioned and re-checked, and the review covers the whole population
rather than a sample. A signed-off review where nothing was ever removed is a
signal that nobody looked.

## Authentication and privilege

- **Multi-factor authentication** on anything internet-facing and on all
  administrative access. Its absence on a remote-access path is a finding on
  almost every framework.
- **Privileged access management**: administrative rights granted just in time,
  for a bounded window, through a checkout that logs the session, rather than
  standing admin accounts.
- **Access models**: RBAC assigns entitlements to roles and roles to people, and
  is the practical default. ABAC decides access from attributes (department,
  location, data tag) at request time, and suits large or highly dynamic
  estates at the cost of more design effort.

## Where IAM fails

**Leaver access left live.** The account is disabled weeks late, or in the
directory but not in three SaaS tools that provision separately. Every access
review starts by hunting for these.

**Privilege creep through moves.** Nobody removes on role change, so long-tenured
staff hold access spanning every team they have passed through: the classic
segregation-of-duties breach.

**Rubber-stamp reviews.** The owner approves 200 lines in one click, removes
nothing, and the review is filed. It satisfies the letter of the control and
none of its purpose.

**Shared accounts.** A `db-admin` login used by four engineers. When something
breaks at 2am, the logs cannot say who did it.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is identity and access management?

The discipline of managing digital identities and what they are permitted to do:
creating and removing accounts, assigning the minimum access each role needs,
enforcing strong authentication, and periodically proving the access that exists
is still appropriate. It underpins almost every other security and compliance
control.

### What is the joiner-mover-leaver process?

The lifecycle of a person's access: provisioned on joining from an approved role
template, adjusted on a role change with old rights removed as new ones are
added, and fully revoked on leaving. The mover and leaver steps are where most
access findings originate, because removal is the part that gets skipped.

### What is the difference between an access review and recertification?

They are the same control under two names: a periodic check where the owner of a
system confirms each person's access is still required and removes what is not.
"Recertification" is the term common in identity-governance tooling;
"access review" or "user access review" is the term auditors use.

### What is least privilege?

The principle that an identity is granted only the access its function requires,
for only as long as it requires it, and nothing more. It limits the damage a
compromised or misused account can do and makes access reviews tractable,
because there is less to justify.
:::

:::lede
Governance answers one question in four artefacts: who decided this, on what
authority, and where is it written down.
:::

The test of a governance function is not whether it meets. It is whether, six
months later, somebody can find the decision, the person who made it, and the
authority they held when they made it.

## The four artefacts

:::terms
The policy set :: The written rules and the standards beneath them. See [2.1](/governance/policy-hierarchy/) for the three levels and why collapsing them is the standard failure.
The committee calendar :: Which forums exist, what each one decides, who sits on it, and when it meets. A committee with no terms of reference is a meeting.
The delegated authority matrix :: Who may approve what, up to what value or severity, without going higher. The artefact that turns "escalate if significant" into something a person can act on at 4pm on a Friday.
The board pack :: What the governing body is told, in what form, on what cycle. See [2.3](/governance/board-reporting/).
:::

## Governance is not management

The distinction is the one COSO and the NIST CSF's Govern function both turn on.
Management runs the organisation. Governance sets the direction management runs
in, and holds it to account for the result.

Practically: management decides how to close a gap; governance decides whether
the gap is acceptable in the first place, and hears about it either way. When
the same forum does both, the challenge disappears — the group approving the
plan is the group that wrote it.

## What a decision record looks like

The minimum that survives a year: the question put, the options considered, the
decision, the person or forum that made it, the date, and the authority relied
on. Six lines.

Most governance failures found in audit are not bad decisions. They are good
decisions with no record of who made them, discovered at the moment somebody
needs to know whether they were entitled to.

:::def term="Delegated authority" ref="2.2"
The specific power a role holds to decide without escalating, stated with its
limit. An authority with no limit is not delegated — it is assumed.
:::

## Where it goes wrong at small scale

A twenty-person company does not need four committees. It needs the four
artefacts to exist in some form, and one of them is usually a shared document
rather than a governance platform.

The failure at this size is not absent governance; it is undocumented
governance. The founders genuinely do decide things, consistently, on defensible
grounds — and none of it is written down, so the first customer security review
finds a company that cannot show it governs anything.

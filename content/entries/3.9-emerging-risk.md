:::lede
An emerging risk is one whose **likelihood or impact is not yet understood well
enough to score reliably**: new, fast-changing or not yet materialised. That is
exactly why it resists the ordinary register, which assumes every risk can
already be sized.
:::

The label is useful and abused in roughly equal measure. Held to its meaning, it
names the small set of risks that a scoring scale genuinely cannot yet handle.
Used loosely, it becomes a place to put anything nobody wants to treat.

## Why they resist the register

A likelihood-times-impact score needs a basis to stand on: history, comparable
events or documented criteria to reason from. An emerging risk has none of these
yet: that absence is what makes it emerging. Force a number onto it and the
number manufactures a precision that does not exist.

The failure is not that the register rejects the risk. It is that the register
absorbs it. An unfamiliar risk with no track record almost always scores low,
and a low score on an ordinary register (see
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/)) is where a risk goes to be forgotten.
Nothing looks wrong. The item is on the list, near the bottom, never reviewed.

## Horizon scanning without theatre

Horizon scanning earns its place only when it feeds a decision. Three things
separate a real scan from a slideshow.

**Named sources.** Regulatory pipelines: consultations and draft legislation
before they bind. Incidents in the sector, especially at comparable
organisations. Technology shifts that change what an attacker or a competitor
can do. Each source has one person responsible for reading it.

**A fixed cadence.** A scan that happens when someone remembers is not a scan.
Quarterly is common; the interval matters less than that it is booked and
minuted.

**A watch-list, held apart.** Emerging risks sit on a list of their own, kept
separate from the register so they are not forced into a score, but put in front
of the same committee that reads the register. An item is promoted into the
register the moment it becomes scorable: the first comparable incident, the
first draft rule with a date attached.

:::def term="Horizon scanning" ref="3.1"
The routine of looking past the current register at signals that may become
risks: regulatory pipelines, sector incidents and technology shifts. Its output
is a dated watch-list, not a score.
:::

## The two failure modes

Two things go wrong, and they pull in opposite directions.

**The label as a hiding place.** A risk that is perfectly scorable gets called
emerging so that no owner, no treatment and no date have to attach to it.
Emerging quietly becomes a synonym for untreated. The test is blunt but not
absolute: if a close comparable has already played out somewhere, the risk can
usually be sized from it, and calling it emerging is often just a way to avoid
doing so. The honest exception is a familiar risk whose scale, context or
trajectory has shifted enough that the old comparables no longer size it.

**The scan nobody acts on.** The opposite failure. A tidy watch-list is produced
each quarter, noted and filed. Nothing is ever promoted, and nothing is ever
struck off as no longer credible. A scan with no route into the register and no
route off the list is theatre with minutes.

:::faq
### What is the difference between an emerging risk and an existing one?
An existing risk can be sized. There is history, criteria or a comparable event
to reason from, so it carries a likelihood, an impact and a place on the
register. An emerging risk cannot be sized yet: it is new, moving too fast to
pin down, or has not materialised anywhere. The dividing line is not how novel
it feels but whether a reliable score is possible.

### How do you score an emerging risk?
Ideally, not yet; that is the whole point. Scoring it prematurely invents a
number with no basis and usually parks it low. Instead, hold it on a watch-list
with what is known, what would change the picture and what is being watched for.
Promote it into the register, and score it, the moment there is a basis: a
comparable incident, a draft rule with a date, a first loss.

### Who owns horizon scanning?
The risk function owns the process: the sources, the cadence and the watch-list.
It does not own the subject-matter judgement behind each entry. Reading the
regulatory pipeline sits with compliance or legal; reading sector incidents sits
with security or operations. See
[3.0](/risk/what-the-risk-function-produces/) for the split between owning a
process and owning the content that runs through it.
:::

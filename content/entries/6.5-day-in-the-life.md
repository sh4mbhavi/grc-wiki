:::lede
Most of a GRC analyst's week is turning other people's work into a record
somebody can act on months later, when the person who did the work is not in the
room. It is writing-heavy, meeting-heavy and deadline-driven, and for a typical
compliance-facing seat far less of it is hands-on technical than people expect.
:::

The rhythm is a steady cadence of recurring work with periodic spikes around
audits and questionnaires, not a queue of tickets. Knowing which kind of work is
in front of you is most of managing the week.

## The recurring work

Some things come round on a cycle whether or not anything has happened.

:::terms
The register :: Keeping risk entries current — chasing owners for updates, moving review dates, flagging anything that has drifted past its date. The register is only as trustworthy as its most stale row.
Evidence :: Collecting the artefacts that show a control operated — an access review, a change record, a backup test — and filing them so they are legible to someone who was not there. The habit, not the collection, is the skill.
Policy upkeep :: Nudging policies through their review dates, tracking exceptions and waivers, keeping the set consistent when one document changes.
Access and change reviews :: The routine control checks that recur monthly or quarterly, where the work is noticing the exception in a list that is mostly fine.
:::

## The triggered work

Then there is the work that arrives, usually with a date attached.

A **security questionnaire** from a prospect's procurement team, which has to be
answered accurately and fast because a deal is waiting on it. An **audit
window**, where weeks of collected evidence get assembled, gaps get closed under
time pressure, and the analyst is the person who knows where everything is. A
**new obligation** — a contract clause, a regulation, a customer requirement —
that has to be mapped to a control before anyone can say whether it is met.

The triggered work is where the recurring work pays off: an analyst who kept the
evidence current all quarter has a calm audit week, and one who did not has a bad
one.

## The one hard conversation

Nearly every week contains a version of the same conversation: a control owner
who has not produced what you asked for. The skill is not escalation — it is
finding out why first (usually the request was ambiguous or the system cannot
produce it), making the ask specific and easy, and keeping a record. Most of the
job's friction lives in this exchange, and handling it well is what separates a
useful analyst from an irritating one.

## What surprises people

Three things, consistently. It is **writing-heavy** — the output is documents and
records, not configurations. For a typical compliance-facing seat it is **not
hands-on technical**; you read systems and challenge answers rather than build
them, though analysts embedded with a cloud, identity or data team do need that
depth. And it is **deadline-driven in bursts** rather than uniformly busy, with
quiet cadence weeks and intense audit weeks. The tools are a GRC platform, a spreadsheet and a ticketing system —
the platform matters less than the discipline behind what goes into it.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is GRC work boring?

It is administrative in stretches and it is not a coding job, so people expecting
constant technical problem-solving find the cadence work slow. What keeps it
engaging for the people who stay is the judgement — deciding which risks matter,
handling the control-owner conversation, seeing the whole organisation's exposure
in a way few other seats do.

### What does a GRC analyst do all day?

Keeps the register current, collects and files evidence, answers security
questionnaires, runs recurring control checks, and chases the people whose work
those records depend on. Most of the day is turning other people's activity into
a record that will hold up later.

### How technical is a GRC analyst role?

It depends on the seat. A typical compliance-facing analyst needs to be literate
rather than hands-on: read a system diagram, understand what a control does, and
challenge an engineer's answer without accepting it whole, without configuring
the system or writing code. An analyst embedded with a cloud or platform team may
need real technical depth — cloud IAM, logging, scripting. See
[6.0](/career/grc-roles/) for how that changes across the seats.
:::

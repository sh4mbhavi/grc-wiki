:::lede
A mapping records which controls satisfy which obligations. It is the artefact
that makes a second framework cost a fraction of the first, and the one most
programmes build last and regret.
:::

The relationship is many-to-many in both directions. One control commonly
satisfies six obligations; one obligation commonly needs three controls. Any
model that assumes otherwise produces a mapping that has to be rebuilt.

## What a row contains

:::terms
Control ID :: Stable, and not the framework's. Borrow ISO's A.5.15 as your control ID and you will renumber the whole inventory when you add SOC 2.
Control statement :: What is done, in the present tense, specific enough to test. "Access is reviewed" is not testable; "privileged access to production is reviewed quarterly by the system owner" is.
Owner :: The named person who operates it. Distinct from the risk owner.
Frequency :: How often it runs, which determines how many evidence instances an auditor will expect.
Obligations satisfied :: The list, with citations. This is the mapping.
Evidence :: What the control produces, and where it lands.
:::

## Building it without losing a month

Start from the controls, not the frameworks. List what the organisation already
does - the access review, the change approval, the backup test, the onboarding
checklist - and write each as a testable statement with an owner.

Then take the framework you have been asked for and walk its requirements
against that list. Three things fall out: controls that map cleanly, controls
you have that nothing requires, and requirements nothing covers. The third list
is your gap analysis, and it arrives as a by-product rather than as a separate
six-week exercise.

Adding the second framework then takes days rather than months, because you are
walking a second requirement set against an inventory that already exists.

## Two failure modes

**Mapping to the framework's structure.** If your inventory is organised as
Annex A, then SOC 2 has to be bolted on sideways, and NIST CSF cannot be
expressed at all. Organise by what you do; map outward.

**One-to-one mapping.** Filling every requirement with exactly one control
produces controls invented to satisfy a requirement rather than to manage a
risk - and an inventory full of controls nobody runs. Some requirements are
satisfied by three controls; some by a documented justification for exclusion.

:::def term="Compensating control" ref="5.3"
A different control accepted as meeting an obligation the prescribed control
would have met, with a documented rationale. Legitimate, and audited harder than
the original.
:::

## Keeping it current

The mapping decays at every framework revision and every reorganisation. Two
habits keep it alive: review it when a control owner changes rather than
annually, and treat a new customer contract clause as an obligation-register
event rather than a legal one.

The second is the one that catches programmes out. A security schedule agreed by
the sales team in March is an obligation from March, and the first anybody in
compliance hears of it is usually the audit.

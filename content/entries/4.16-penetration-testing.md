:::lede
A penetration test is an **authorised, time-boxed, scoped attempt to exploit
weaknesses in a system** the way a real attacker would — chaining findings
together to reach a defined objective and demonstrate concrete impact, not just
list what is theoretically wrong.
:::

The word most people get wrong is *exploit*. A vulnerability scan reports that a
door looks unlocked. A penetration test opens it, walks through, and shows what
is in the room. That difference — proven impact versus flagged presence — is why
a framework such as PCI DSS mandates a real test annually and does not accept a
scan in its place.

A penetration test is a point-in-time assurance activity, not a security
programme. It tells you what an attacker could have done to that scope, on that
date, with that much time. It does not keep the system secure between tests;
[vulnerability management](/compliance/vulnerability-management/) does that.

## Test against scan

:::attrs a="Penetration test" b="Vulnerability scan"
Method :: Mostly manual, human-driven :: Automated tool
Goal :: Reach an objective, prove impact :: Enumerate known weaknesses
Chains findings :: +Yes — several low issues into one critical path :: -No — each finding stands alone
False positives :: Low, findings are verified by exploitation :: Higher, needs triage
Frequency :: Annually and on major change :: Continuously or weekly
Output :: Narrative of attack paths, rated by real impact :: List of CVEs by severity
:::

:::def term="Red team exercise" ref="4.15"
A broader, objective-based engagement testing detection and response as well as
prevention, usually without the defenders knowing, over weeks rather than days.
A penetration test asks "can this be broken into"; a red team asks "would we
notice, and could we stop it".
:::

## Scoping and rules of engagement

The test is only as useful as its scope, and the scope is a governance
artefact, not a technical detail.

- **Targets**: the exact systems, applications, IP ranges and accounts in and
  out of bounds. A test scoped to one application will not find the flat network
  behind it — that has to be a deliberate choice, recorded.
- **Knowledge level**: black box (the tester starts blind), grey box (given
  low-privilege credentials and some documentation) or white box (full access to
  source and design). Grey box usually returns the most findings per day of
  effort, because the tester does not spend the budget on reconnaissance.
- **Rules of engagement**: permitted hours, techniques excluded (denial of
  service, social engineering, physical), the emergency contact, and explicit
  written authorisation from someone who owns the systems. Testing without that
  authorisation is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions.
- **Handling of live data and third parties**: cloud providers and SaaS vendors
  often require advance notice or forbid testing of shared infrastructure
  entirely.

## Reading the report

The finding count is not the headline. What matters is the **attack narrative**:
the path from external position to the objective, and the one or two links in
that chain that, if broken, would have stopped it. A report with thirty
low-severity findings and no viable path is a better result than one with three
lows that chain into domain admin.

Then the retest. Findings are remediated, the tester re-checks the specific
issues, and a retest report records what closed and what did not. A penetration
test with no retest leaves the assurance question half-answered.

## Where penetration testing fails

**Scoped to nothing.** The scope is narrowed until the test cannot find anything
serious, then the clean report is shown to customers as proof of security.

**Scan sold as a test.** An automated scan with light manual validation is
delivered as a "penetration test" at test prices. PCI assessors and mature
customers can tell the difference from the report.

**No remediation or retest.** The report is filed, the findings are not tracked
in the risk register, and the same issues appear in next year's test.

**Point-in-time treated as continuous.** The annual test passes and the
organisation assumes it is secure for twelve months, with no vulnerability
management running in between.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is the difference between a penetration test and a vulnerability scan?

A vulnerability scan is an automated check that lists known weaknesses. A
penetration test is a mostly manual engagement where a tester actively exploits
weaknesses, chains them together and demonstrates real impact against a defined
objective. Scans run continuously and produce a list; tests run periodically and
produce an attack narrative.

### How often should penetration testing be done?

At least annually, and after any significant change to the environment — a new
application, a major architecture change, a migration. Regulated regimes are
specific: PCI DSS requires external and internal testing at least annually and
after significant change. High-risk or fast-changing systems may warrant more
frequent testing.

### What is the difference between black box, grey box and white box testing?

They describe how much the tester is told. Black box: no prior knowledge, closest
to an external attacker but slow. White box: full access to source, configuration
and design, most thorough per unit of time. Grey box: limited credentials and
some documentation, and usually the most cost-effective for application testing.

### Is a penetration test the same as a red team exercise?

No. A penetration test aims to find and prove exploitable weaknesses in a defined
scope, usually openly and over days. A red team exercise is objective-driven,
covert, runs over weeks, and tests whether the security team detects and responds
as well as whether the controls hold.
:::

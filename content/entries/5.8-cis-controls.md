:::lede
The **CIS Controls** are a prioritised, prescriptive set of cyber-defence actions maintained by the Center for Internet Security, a non-profit. Version 8 organises 18 controls into 153 measurable safeguards, ordered so an organisation knows not just what to do but in what order to do it.
:::

Formerly the SANS Top 20 and later the CIS Critical Security Controls, the set has been renamed more than once but keeps the same job: tell a defender which actions cut the most risk first. It is free to adopt and certifies nothing. Where an outcomes framework such as [NIST CSF](/frameworks/nist-csf-2/) (5.5) states results to achieve and leaves the controls to you, the CIS Controls hand you the actions and the order — which is why they read less like a standard to interpret and more like a roadmap to work through. Read them against [how to read a framework](/frameworks/how-to-read-a-framework/) (5.0) before adopting.

## The 18 controls and 153 safeguards

Version 8, published in 2021, introduced the structure the Controls still use. It cut the count from 20 controls in version 7 to 18 and made the safeguard — not the control — the unit that matters. A control is a theme; a safeguard is one specific, measurable action inside it, and version 8 defines 153 of them.

:::terms
Control :: A theme or category of defensive activity — 18 in version 8 — such as inventory of enterprise assets or data protection.
Safeguard :: A single, measurable action inside a control. The safeguard, not the control, is what gets an owner and evidence; version 8 holds 153.
:::

The reorganisation in version 8 is the change worth understanding. Earlier versions grouped controls partly by who owned the asset. Version 8 groups them by the activity being performed, so a single control now spans whatever devices, users or cloud services that activity touches — which matches how a modern estate actually runs. A later refresh, version 8.1 (2024), realigned the mappings to [NIST CSF](/frameworks/nist-csf-2/) 2.0 and sharpened the governance and asset-class framing.

## Implementation Groups: choose a tier by risk

Not every enterprise is expected to implement all 153 safeguards. Implementation Groups — IG1, IG2 and IG3 — are a prioritisation overlay that sorts every safeguard into a tier, so an organisation can start with what matters most and add the rest as its risk and resources justify.

The groups are nested. IG1 is a subset of IG2, which is a subset of IG3, and every safeguard is assigned to the lowest group that requires it. IG1 is the foundational baseline — CIS calls it essential cyber hygiene — and holds 56 of the 153 safeguards in version 8.

| Group | What it is | Chosen when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IG1 | Essential cyber hygiene; the 56-safeguard baseline every enterprise should meet | Always — it is the floor |
| IG2 | IG1 plus safeguards for more sensitive data and more complex operations | Regulated or sensitive data is held across departments |
| IG3 | IG2 plus the remaining safeguards | The organisation must withstand sophisticated, targeted attacks |

:::def term="Implementation Group 1 (IG1)" ref="5.9"
The foundational tier of the CIS Controls — 56 safeguards CIS calls essential cyber hygiene — meant as the baseline every enterprise should meet. It is chosen by an organisation's resources and data sensitivity, not climbed towards: IG1 done well is a legitimate end state, not a rung below IG3. Distinct from a maturity level, which rates how well a control is performed rather than which safeguards are in scope.
:::

Which group you target is a risk decision, not a maturity climb. It is set by the organisation's resources and the sensitivity of the data it holds — not by an ambition to reach IG3. A small business that meets IG1 well has arrived somewhere legitimate, not paused partway up a ladder. Reading IG3 as the destination is the most common way the model is misused, and it is close kin to the mistake of treating [control maturity](/frameworks/control-maturity/) (5.9) as a ladder to level 5.

## Prescriptive and prioritised, not a catalogue

What distinguishes the CIS Controls from most other frameworks is that they tell you what to do and in what order. A large control catalogue such as [NIST SP 800-53](/frameworks/nist-800-53/) (5.7) gives you a superset to select and tailor from; an outcomes framework such as [NIST CSF](/frameworks/nist-csf-2/) (5.5) states results and leaves the mechanism open. The CIS Controls sit at the prescriptive, prioritised end: the safeguards are the actions, and the Implementation Groups set the priority.

| Aspect | CIS Controls | NIST 800-53 | NIST CSF |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Form | Prioritised action list | Control catalogue | Outcome statements |
| Tells you | What to do, in what order | The full menu to tailor | The result to achieve |
| First move | Work IG1 | Pick a baseline, then tailor | Set target outcomes |

Prescriptive does not mean isolated. CIS publishes mappings from the Controls to NIST CSF, NIST 800-53, ISO/IEC 27001, PCI DSS and others, so the set can act as an implementation-ordered layer that crosswalks to whichever compliance framework you owe. You work the safeguards in priority order, and the mapping shows which obligations each one helps satisfy — a way to sequence hardening while still tracking against the standard you are held to.

## Controls are not Benchmarks

Two CIS products are constantly confused. The CIS Controls tell you what to do. The CIS Benchmarks are a separate product — secure-configuration guidance for specific technologies, such as a given operating system, browser or cloud service, down to individual settings. A control might say to configure enterprise assets securely; the matching Benchmark is the setting-by-setting document telling you how to harden a particular version of a particular system. Both come from CIS. They answer different questions, and neither is a substitute for the other.

## Where it goes wrong

**IGs treated as a maturity ladder.** The three groups get read as levels to climb, with IG3 as the goal. They are a prioritisation overlay keyed to risk and resources, not rungs — IG1 met properly is a legitimate end state for many organisations, and reaching for IG3 without the data sensitivity or threat exposure to justify it spends effort where the risk does not warrant it.

**Controls confused with Benchmarks.** A team hardens its servers to the CIS Benchmarks and reports that it has "done CIS", or adopts the Controls and assumes its systems are configured. The two are different products answering different questions — what to do versus how to configure one specific system — and neither covers the other's ground.

**IG1 hygiene skipped to chase advanced safeguards.** Attention goes to sophisticated IG3 safeguards while basic hygiene in IG1 — asset inventory, account management and patching — is left incomplete. The safeguards are ordered for a reason: the IG1 baseline blocks the most common attacks for the least effort, and building advanced defences on top of an incomplete baseline leaves the front door open behind them.

**A certificate expected.** Someone asks to see the organisation's "CIS certification". There is none. The Controls are free to adopt and not certifiable; you can self-assess, and CIS offers configuration-assessment tooling such as CIS-CAT that scores systems against the Benchmarks, but none of that is an organisational certification of the kind ISO/IEC 27001 provides.

:::faq
### Do I have to implement all 153 safeguards?
No. The safeguards are tiered into Implementation Groups so you start where your risk and resources put you. IG1, the 56-safeguard essential-hygiene baseline, is the floor every enterprise should meet; IG2 and IG3 add the rest for organisations holding more sensitive data or facing more capable attackers. Meeting IG1 well is a legitimate place to stop, not a partial result.

### Are the CIS Controls the same as the CIS Benchmarks?
No, and conflating them is the most common CIS mistake. The Controls are a prioritised list of what to do across a programme; the Benchmarks are secure-configuration guides for specific technologies — how to harden a particular operating system, browser or cloud service. Both come from CIS, but one sets the actions and the other details the settings for a single system.

### Can my organisation get CIS certified?
No. There is no CIS certification for an organisation. The Controls are free to adopt and you can assess yourself against them; CIS also sells assessment tooling like CIS-CAT that checks systems against the Benchmarks. None of that is an accredited certificate — if you need one, it comes from a framework such as ISO/IEC 27001, which the Controls can be mapped to.
:::

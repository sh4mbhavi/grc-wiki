:::lede
GRC, governance, risk and compliance, is the most beginner-friendly part of
cyber security: no coding, no networking and no multi-year experience bar on
entry-level roles. It is also among the least exposed to automation, because the
work is largely human: assessing risk, running audits and explaining both to
stakeholders.
:::

Most people miss these jobs for three reasons. They do not know the roles exist,
they do not know which titles to search, or they lose months on a low-quality
course that teaches nothing and give up. This entry follows the roadmap in the
video linked at the foot: what the roles are, how to learn the function, how to
write it up and which certifications are worth the money.

## What GRC roles look like

The term is recent; the work is not. Present-day GRC roles fall into three
groups.

:::terms
Risk management :: Risk assessments and analysis on projects, applications and initiatives, then documenting the risk and communicating it to stakeholders. Split across the first and second lines of the three lines model.
Audit :: Auditing a security programme, from inside an internal audit function, as an external auditor, or against a named framework such as ISO 27001.
Consulting and strategy :: Senior work: long-term strategy, architectural assessment and the risk narrative a board hears. Not hands-on with tools. The chief information security officer role is essentially this, with people management on top.
:::

Entry-level hiring is almost all in the first two groups.

## Step 1: learn the function, not networking

The foundation for GRC is the GRC function itself, covered end to end. Working
from the video's course outline, that means:

- risk management: purpose, identifying threats, running an assessment, risk registers, the CIA triad, the OWASP Top 10, privacy, and where organisations get it wrong
- audit: what an audit is, and the three lines model
- asset management: identifying and classifying assets, and the usual failure points
- identity and access: authentication, multi-factor, authorisation, from a GRC view rather than an engineering one
- security education and awareness
- data loss prevention and data security: classification and labelling
- detection and incident response: the role of a SIEM, managed providers, the incident lifecycle and the MITRE ATT&CK framework
- third-party risk management
- how penetration testing and vulnerability management fit a programme, and when they do not

A course earns its price here by being practical: assessments and case studies
built on real company material, not slides read aloud. The video's own course,
GRC Mastery, is structured this way and assumes no technical background; it is
compared with the alternatives in [6.3](/career/certifications-and-training/).

## Step 2: build a portfolio while you learn

Each topic above should end in a small worked artefact. The projects worth
putting on a CV, per the video:

- a full ISMS built from scratch: risk assessments, a statement of applicability and the core policies and procedures an ISO 27001 certification needs
- an end-to-end assessment of an organisation using the NIST Cybersecurity Framework
- a third-party risk assessment, a skill the video notes is rare in candidates
- an asset-management process that captures assets and maintains a CMDB

Policy writing and risk assessment transfer to any framework, so the ISMS
project is not only about ISO 27001.

## Step 3: write it up for the screener

Two rules from the video:

- Spell out "governance, risk and compliance", not just an acronym or a course name, so an applicant-tracking system and a hiring manager both read it. Name the recognised skills underneath: risk management, audit, asset management, identity and access, education and awareness, third-party risk.
- List three or four projects with one line each, leading with the ISMS and the NIST CSF assessment, then the third-party and asset-management pieces. Each line dense with the terms a screener searches for.

A free CV template that pre-fills these projects is linked from the video.

## Step 4: find the jobs

Most GRC roles do not have "GRC" in the title, so search differently:

- framework names: "ISO 27001", "NIST"
- the phrases "cyber security risk" and "cyber security audit"
- then plain "cyber", read description by description; the GRC roles hidden there attract fewer applicants

Titles to expect include information system security officer and information
security officer. Use LinkedIn plus one job board specific to your country.

Apply when you meet roughly a fifth of the advert. Adverts are wish lists, and
because frameworks overlap heavily, experience with one such as ISO 27001
carries into a role asking for another such as HIPAA or SOC 2. Do not screen
yourself out.

## Certifications, and what to skip

Covered in full at [6.3](/career/certifications-and-training/). In short, from
the video:

- Skip theory-only certificates built around the letters "GRC". The industry does not weigh them and they do not teach the work.
- Worth having alongside a practical course: a broad introduction such as the Google Cybersecurity Certificate; CompTIA Security+, which is a US Department of Defense requirement for some government roles and optional otherwise; and a Microsoft path leading into the SC-900 exam for cloud, security and compliance fundamentals.
- With several years of experience, ISACA's CISA or CISM, or ISC2's CISSP, add breadth, though none is GRC-specific.

## Technical add-ons

GRC does not configure tools, but the more you understand them the more roles
you fit. The video's optional additions:

- cloud fundamentals: Microsoft Azure Fundamentals or the AWS Cloud Practitioner, an introduction rather than an engineering track
- basic SOC analyst skills through a hands-on pathway, which can open generalist roles that mix GRC with light monitoring work

Small and mid-size organisations cannot afford specialists, so a generalist who
can run a risk assessment, run an audit and respond to a phishing report is
highly employable.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Do you need a degree or coding to get into GRC?

No. GRC is the part of cyber security that does not touch networking or code.
Entry-level roles also lack the multi-year experience requirements and expensive
exams attached to technical roles such as penetration testing.

### Why do so few people apply for GRC jobs?

Three reasons from the video: they do not know the roles exist, they do not know
which job titles to search because most do not contain "GRC", or they spend
months on a weak course and quit before applying.

### What job titles should I search for?

Framework names such as "ISO 27001" and "NIST", the phrases "cyber security
risk" and "cyber security audit", and then plain "cyber" read description by
description. Titles such as information system security officer and information
security officer are often GRC roles.

### How much of a job advert do I need to match?

About a fifth. Adverts are wish lists, and because frameworks overlap,
experience with one carries into roles asking for another. Apply and let the
employer decide.
:::

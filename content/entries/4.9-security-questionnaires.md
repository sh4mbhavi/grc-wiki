:::lede
A security questionnaire is a **standardised set of questions used to assess a
vendor's security posture** — the SIG and the CAIQ are the common templates —
and it is a diligence artefact, not assurance in itself.
:::

The questionnaire is where third-party diligence usually starts, and it is where
weak programmes stop. Treating the returned document as the assessment confuses
the instrument for the finding: three hundred answered questions with nothing
sampled behind them tell you what a vendor is willing to assert, not what is true.

## Two jobs, not one

The same document sits on two desks, and most people only ever meet one side of it.

**Sending.** As part of third-party diligence, the questionnaire goes out to a
prospective or existing vendor to probe its security posture before and during
the relationship. It is one instrument of the wider
[vendor due diligence](/risk/third-party-risk/) at 3.8, not the whole of it — a
completed questionnaire narrows what still needs verifying, it does not close the
assessment.

**Answering.** As the vendor being assessed, you complete other organisations'
questionnaires to win and keep their business. For a company that sells to
enterprises this is a standing workload measured in hundreds of questions a
quarter, and it turns into a revenue-blocking task the moment a deal waits on it.

## Why a completed questionnaire is not assurance

The answers are self-attested. A vendor asserting that access is reviewed
quarterly and data is encrypted at rest has told you what it believes to be true;
it has shown you nothing. Assurance arrives only with the artefact behind the
answer — an independent audit report such as SOC 2, an ISO 27001 certificate
scoped to the service you are buying or an actual configuration export.

A "yes" with nothing behind it is a claim, not a control. A SOC 2 report and an
ISO 27001 certificate answer different questions — see
[5.1](/frameworks/iso-27001-vs-soc-2/) — and a certificate scoped to the wrong
entity proves nothing about the service in front of you. The questionnaire tells
you what to verify; the [evidence](/compliance/evidence-that-holds-up/) is what
verifies it.

## Tier the questionnaire to the risk

Sending a three-hundred-question SIG to a vendor that stores none of your data
and touches none of your systems is questionnaire theatre: it burns the vendor's
time and yours, and nobody reads the result. Depth of questionnaire should follow
the risk tier set in [third-party risk management](/risk/third-party-risk/) — a
lightweight set for a routine supplier, the full instrument reserved for a vendor
inside a recovery time objective or holding production data. The SIG ships in
exactly this spirit, with a shorter SIG Lite for lower-tier suppliers and the
full SIG Core for the ones that can actually hurt you.

| Dimension | SIG | CAIQ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Author | Shared Assessments | Cloud Security Alliance |
| Scope | Broad third-party risk across many control domains | Cloud service security, mapped to the Cloud Controls Matrix |
| Form | Configurable question set, from SIG Core down to SIG Lite | Yes/no control assertions against CCM domains |
| Standing | Licensed content set, maintained and updated | Freely available, underpinning CSA STAR registry listings |

## The answer library

Redrafting the same three hundred questions by hand every time is how the
workload becomes unbearable and how the answers go stale. The fix is a reviewed
knowledge base — every standard question with an approved answer and a link to
the evidence behind it — that a response can be assembled from and then tailored
to the asker.

It needs a named owner who keeps it current. An answer library nobody maintains
is a catalogue of last year's claims, and the drift is invisible until a
customer's auditor asks to see the control behind an answer that stopped being
true two releases ago.

## Where questionnaires fail

**A completed questionnaire treated as assurance.** The document comes back, gets
filed and closes the assessment, with no report, certificate or export sampled
behind a single answer. The programme has collected assertions and recorded them
as facts.

**Answers that drift from reality.** The questionnaire is refreshed once a year
while the environment changes every week. Between refreshes the answers describe a
posture the organisation no longer has, and nobody notices until an incident or an
audit finds the gap.

**Questionnaire theatre.** Hundreds of questions issued and returned, none
validated. The volume signals diligence and delivers none — a longer questionnaire
that nobody checks is weaker than a short one that is verified.

**One questionnaire for every tier.** A low-risk vendor is sent the same
instrument as a critical one, so the routine supplier is over-assessed and the
critical one is assessed no harder than the rest. Effort decouples from risk, and
the review that mattered gets the same cursory read as the ninety that did not.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is a security questionnaire?
A standardised set of questions an organisation sends a vendor to assess its
security posture, or receives and completes when it is the vendor being assessed.
The SIG and the CAIQ are the two common templates. It is a diligence artefact — a
structured way to ask — and not assurance in itself, because the answers are
self-attested until evidence is produced behind them.

### What is the difference between a SIG and a CAIQ?
The SIG (Standardized Information Gathering, from Shared Assessments) is a broad
third-party-risk questionnaire covering many control domains, licensed as a
maintained content set and configurable from a full SIG Core down to a shorter
SIG Lite. The CAIQ (Consensus Assessments Initiative Questionnaire, from the Cloud
Security Alliance) is narrower and cloud-specific: yes/no control assertions
mapped to the Cloud Controls Matrix and freely available, and a submitted CAIQ is
what backs a Level 1 listing in the CSA STAR registry. Use the SIG for general
supplier diligence; expect the CAIQ from a cloud provider.

### Is a completed security questionnaire enough assurance?
No. A completed questionnaire is a set of claims, not evidence they are true.
Assurance comes from what stands behind the answers — an independent audit report
such as SOC 2, a certification such as ISO 27001 scoped to the service in question
or a direct artefact like a configuration export. Treat the questionnaire as the
thing that tells you what to verify, then verify the answers that matter.
:::

:::def term="Vendor due diligence" ref="3.8"
The assessment of a supplier's security, financial and regulatory standing before
and during the relationship, scaled to the risk tier. A questionnaire is one
instrument of that diligence, not the whole of it — verifying what the answers
claim is the rest.
:::

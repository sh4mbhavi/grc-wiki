:::lede
Governance and management divide along **deciding versus doing**: governance sets
the direction, the risk appetite and the lines of accountability; management runs
the organisation within them. They are distinct functions — usually held by the
board and the executive respectively — not two words for one, so that the people
who set the target are not the sole judges of whether it was hit.
:::

The separation is the whole point, and it is the first thing to erode. A board
that starts approving individual purchases has stopped governing; an executive
that starts deciding how much risk is acceptable has started governing without a
mandate. Both are ordinary, and both are named at the foot of this entry.

When something goes wrong, the first useful question is which of the two failed,
because the remedies do not overlap. A governance failure is answered by changing
a strategy, an appetite or a person on the board; a management failure is
answered by changing how the work is run. Blur the two and the right fix lands on
the wrong body.

## The comparison

:::versus a="Governance" a-badge="THE BOARD" b="Management" b-badge="THE EXECUTIVE"
Sets the direction, fixes the risk appetite, appoints the executive and holds it
to account. Owns the destination and runs nothing itself.
+++
Runs the organisation day to day within the direction and appetite the board set,
and reports back on whether it is working. Owns the journey and sets nothing
above itself.
:::

:::attrs a="Governance" b="Management"
Who (typically) :: The board, largely non-executive, acting collectively :: The executive — the CEO and the management team
Time horizon :: The long term — strategy, appetite, succession :: The operating cycle — this quarter, this year
Decides :: Direction, risk appetite, who holds authority :: How to execute within that direction
Accountable for :: Oversight and outcomes, to stakeholders :: Delivery and daily operation, to the board
Primary output :: Approved strategy, appetite statement, terms of reference :: Operating results, running controls, reports up
Cadence :: Periodic — the board and committee calendar :: Continuous — the working day
Fails when :: It drifts into running the operation :: It sets the appetite it is judged against
:::

The *Accountable for* row does the real work. Both bodies answer to someone, but
not to the same someone and not for the same thing. Management answers to the
board for delivery; the board answers to shareholders, members or the public for
whether the organisation was directed and overseen well. ISO 37000 puts this at
the centre of governance and draws the line most people miss: responsibility can
be delegated to management, but accountability is retained by the governing body.
The board can hand over the running of the place; it cannot hand over answering
for it.

## Why the separation exists

Two bodies exist rather than one because of separation of duties, applied at the
top of the house. It is the same principle that stops the person who raises an
invoice from being the person who approves it — whoever sets a target should not
also be the only judge of whether it was met. Fold direction-setting and delivery
into one pair of hands and the appetite quietly bends to fit the results.

The [three lines model](/foundations/three-lines-model/) draws the same line
lower down — the governing body above, management owning the first and second
lines, independent assurance in the third — and the whole arrangement rests on the
governing body being distinct from the management it oversees. What joins the two
bodies without merging them is delegated authority. The board does not run the
organisation, so it grants the executive the power to, within limits it writes
down and can withdraw, recorded in the
[committee and delegation structure](/governance/committees-and-delegated-authority/)
beneath it.

:::def term="Delegated authority" ref="2.2"
The board's grant of decision-making power to the executive, within limits it
sets and can revoke. The hinge between the two bodies: governance decides what
the organisation is for, then delegates the authority to run it — and keeps the
accountability.
:::

## Where the line blurs

The clean two-body picture assumes a board of non-executives and a separate
executive team, which describes a listed company and not much else.

In an owner-managed business the founder chairs the board on Monday and runs the
operation for the rest of the week, and the two roles share one diary. The
distinction has not vanished; it has become a discipline the individual has to
keep in their own head — knowing, at any given decision, whether they are setting
the appetite or spending it. Small firms fail here not by collapsing the roles
but by never noticing they hold both.

The executive director is the deliberate blur. Most boards seat at least one —
the CEO, often the finance director — as a full member of the governing body who
is also part of the management it oversees. They govern in the boardroom and
manage outside it, which is why a functioning board structures itself so the
executive members cannot dominate the governance decisions: non-executive
majorities, committees chaired by non-executives, sessions the executives leave.

The distinction still matters where one person holds both roles, because
accountability does not merge just because the diary did. When the owner-manager's
company is sued or audited, whether this was a governance failure or a management
failure still has to be answered — by the same person, in the right role, if the
answer is to be worth anything.

## The failure mode

Two failures sit either side of the line, and each is the other's mirror.

The first is a board that drifts into managing. It fills its meetings approving
individual hires, signing off line items and reading operational dashboards —
rubber-stamping the detail the executive was appointed to own. The detail feels
like diligence, but it crowds out the work only the board can do: setting
direction, testing the strategy and holding the executive to account. A board
deep in operational minutiae is usually a board that has quietly stopped
overseeing, because no time is left for it.

The second is an executive that makes governance-level calls without saying so.
The clearest case is an executive that sets its own risk appetite — deciding how
much risk is acceptable, then reporting performance against a bar it set for
itself. COSO puts risk appetite under board oversight for exactly this reason: the
executive may propose it, but the board reviews and concurs, so that the body
measured against the appetite is not the body that fixes it. Left to itself, an
executive can widen the appetite whenever the results demand, and no one above it
is the wiser. It is self-review raised to the level of strategy — the body being
held to account has taken over defining what account looks like.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is governance the same as management?

No. Governance decides what the organisation is for, how much risk it will run
and who holds authority; management operates inside those decisions and reports
back. Same organisation, two jobs — one sets the frame, the other works within
it. The words are used loosely in everyday speech, which is where the confusion
starts, but every governance standard treats them as distinct functions.

### Who actually does governance?

The governing body — in a company, the board and its committees, acting
collectively rather than through the chair alone. In a charity it is the
trustees, in a public body the accountable board. In a very small organisation it
may be one or two people who put the governance hat on at set moments — approving
the annual plan, signing the accounts — and take it off to run the place the rest
of the time. Governance is a role a body performs, not a department on the chart.

### Can a manager do governance work?

Yes, but only when acting in a governance capacity, not by virtue of being
senior. A manager who sits on a governance committee, or the executive director
on the board, is doing governance in that seat — bound by its terms of reference
and its record. The moment they leave the seat they are managing again. What
decides whether a piece of work is governance is the authority it is done under,
not the seniority of the person doing it.
:::

:::lede
A **business impact analysis** works out which activities matter most and how
quickly each must be back, producing the recovery time objective (RTO), recovery
point objective (RPO) and maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD) the whole
continuity plan is sized against.
:::

It is the numbers engine under [business continuity](/risk/business-continuity/).
Everything downstream — recovery strategy, standby capacity, the plan's own
deadlines — is scaled from what the analysis produces. Get the numbers wrong and
the plan is either unaffordable or too slow, and there is no way to tell which
until it is used.

## The three numbers

:::terms
Recovery time objective (RTO) :: The target time to restore an activity after disruption. Miss it and the activity has been down longer than the plan promised anyone it would be.
Recovery point objective (RPO) :: The maximum data loss that is tolerable, expressed as a length of time — the point back to which data must be recoverable. An RPO of fifteen minutes means never losing more than the last fifteen minutes of work.
Maximum tolerable period of disruption (MTPD/MTD) :: The elapsed time after which the impact of not resuming becomes unacceptable. The hard outer boundary the RTO has to sit inside.
:::

The ordering is a constraint, not a preference. RTO must be set shorter than
MTPD. Set them equal, or the wrong way round, and the plan is scheduled to finish
restoring the activity at exactly the moment the damage has already become
unacceptable — it misses its own deadline by design.

:::def term="Recovery point objective" ref="3.6"
The maximum tolerable data loss, stated as a length of time rather than a volume.
It sets how often data must be backed up or replicated: a fifteen-minute RPO
forces backups no more than fifteen minutes apart.
:::

## How a BIA is run

Four steps, in order.

**List the activities.** Every activity the organisation performs, not the
systems behind them. Payroll is an activity; the payroll server is a dependency
of it.

**Grade impact against time.** The same outage is trivial at one hour and fatal
at one week. Impact is assessed as a curve that grows with the length of the
disruption — financial, legal, reputational and safety impact each plotted across
hours, days and weeks. This is the step most often skipped, and skipping it is
what makes the resulting numbers meaningless.

**Map dependencies.** For each activity, the people, systems, suppliers and
premises it needs to run. An activity cannot recover faster than the slowest
thing it depends on, so an RTO shorter than its dependencies' is a fiction.

**Set the numbers from the curve.** The point where impact turns unacceptable
fixes the MTPD, and the RTO is set shorter than it, with margin to spare. The RPO
answers a separate question — how much data the activity can afford to lose — and
is read from the tolerable gap between backups, not off the downtime curve. The
numbers come from the impact, not the other way round. A programme that picks a
comfortable RTO first and writes the impact analysis to justify it afterwards has
the whole process reversed.

## A worked example

Take one activity — customer payment processing — with an RTO of 4 hours, an RPO
of 15 minutes and an MTPD of 24 hours. Each figure forces something concrete.

| Figure | What it forces |
| --- | --- |
| RTO 4 hours | Payments must be recoverable within four hours of an outage, so the standby capacity, failover and trained staff to do it have to exist and be tested. |
| RPO 15 minutes | Transaction data must be captured or replicated at least every fifteen minutes, so no more than a quarter-hour of payments is ever lost. |
| MTPD 24 hours | Past a full day down, customers give up and move on, and the lost revenue stops being recoverable at all. |

The RTO buys margin against the MTPD. Four hours against a twenty-four-hour
tolerance leaves room for a recovery that runs late without crossing the point of
no return. That margin is the reason RTO is set below MTPD rather than at it.

## Where the BIA goes wrong

**Everything is rated critical.** When every activity is top priority, every RTO
collapses to one hour and the plan demands more simultaneous recovery than any
organisation can fund. A BIA that does not force a ranking has not been done.

**Impact is never graded against time.** A single severity score with no time
axis cannot locate the MTPD — the moment impact crosses from tolerable to not —
and without that point there is nothing for the RTO to be set inside. Without the
curve the numbers are guesses wearing a decimal point.

**It is run once and shelved.** A BIA completed for certification and never
revisited describes an organisation that has since moved on — activities,
suppliers and volumes have all changed. It is reviewed at planned intervals and
after any material change to the business — commonly at least once a year in
practice.

:::faq
### What is the difference between RTO and RPO?
RTO looks forward: how quickly the activity must be running again. RPO looks backward: how much data, measured in time, can be lost when it returns. RTO is about downtime, RPO is about data loss, and the two are set independently — a payment system can tolerate hours of downtime but almost no lost transactions.

### Who provides the data for a BIA?
The people who run the activity, not the risk or continuity function. The business owners state what breaks and when it starts to hurt; continuity facilitates, challenges the answers and records them. Numbers set by the continuity team alone are the ones the business disowns during an actual incident.

### How often should a BIA be redone?
At least annually, and on any material change — a new product, an acquisition, a moved supplier or a changed regulatory obligation. A BIA is a snapshot of a moving business; left unrevised it goes quietly out of date and takes the whole continuity plan with it.
:::

:::lede
The titles vary more than the work does. Every seat in this field is
accountable for one or more of the four artefacts, and the useful way to read a
job advert is to work out which.
:::

Salary bands are deliberately omitted here: they move, they are regional, and a
number in a reference work goes stale in a way the rest of an entry does not.
What follows is what each seat actually does.

## The ladder

:::terms
GRC analyst :: The entry seat. Runs the register day to day, chases evidence, completes customer questionnaires, keeps the policy set current. Most of the week is writing things down so somebody else can act on them.
GRC / compliance manager :: Owns a framework end to end and the audit relationship that goes with it. First seat with a budget conversation and the first with a genuine right to say no.
Risk manager :: Owns the register, the appetite statement and the committee reporting. Divergence point: this seat drifts toward the business, the compliance seat toward the auditors.
Head of GRC :: Owns the programme and the board narrative. Answers for the function's opinion rather than its paperwork, and spends more time on resourcing than on controls.
:::

## The four specialisations

**Third-party risk.** The first split in almost every organisation, because the
volume of supplier assessments is relentless and unlike the rest of the work.

**Privacy.** A distinct obligation set with its own regulator, its own artefacts
and — where a statutory DPO is required — independence constraints that make it
a poor fit for a general compliance seat.

**Technology / product compliance.** Sits closest to engineering. Owns the
controls that live in the pipeline, and needs enough fluency to challenge an
architecture decision.

**Financial controls / SOX.** A different lineage entirely, closer to internal
audit and the finance function, with its own qualification path.

## What each seat is measured on

Not by controls implemented, in any function worth working in. Analysts are
measured on whether the artefacts are current and legible. Managers on whether
audits produce surprises. Heads on whether the board's picture of risk matches
the organisation's actual position.

An interview question worth asking in the other direction: *how do you know this
function is working?* An answer in terms of coverage percentages describes a
function that reports on itself. An answer in terms of decisions made and
surprises avoided describes one that is used.

:::def term="Second line" ref="1.5"
The specialist functions providing expertise, challenge and monitoring to the
first line. Almost every GRC seat sits here.
:::

## Where people go next

Sideways more often than up, and that is normal. Risk to privacy, compliance to
internal audit, GRC to security architecture, and — increasingly — GRC into
product, because the person who understands the obligations understands what the
product has to do.

The route out that surprises people is into consulting, where the breadth of a
generalist seat is worth more than the depth of a specialist one.

:::lede
Change management is the **control that changes to production are authorised,
tested and documented before release**, so no change reaches a live system
without a record of who asked, who approved, what was tested and when it went
out.
:::

An unauthorised change to production is a common way a control that looks
healthy on paper turns out not to be operating. It is also among the first
things an auditor tests, because a weak change process quietly undermines every
other control: each of them assumes that production only changes through a path
someone approved.

The value of the control is not the paperwork. It is that the paperwork cannot
be produced honestly after the fact, so a change is either evidenced as
authorised or it is unsupported, and to an auditor an authorisation that cannot
be evidenced is one that did not happen.

## What a change record carries

A valid change leaves a single record that links four things, each attributable
to a named person and each dated by the system rather than typed in afterwards.

:::terms
Request :: What is being changed and why, raised before the work starts. A request opened after deployment is not a request; it is a reconstruction.
Approval :: A named approver who is not the person who built the change, recorded before release. This is the authorisation the whole control exists to capture.
Test result :: Evidence that the change was exercised before it reached production: the test that ran, its outcome and where it ran. "Tested: yes" is not a test result.
Deployment :: When the change went live, who released it and to which system. This is the line that ties the approved change to the thing now running.
:::

Read as a chain, the record answers request, approval, test then deployment for
every change, and an auditor sampling releases reads it in that order. A change
missing any link is the link the finding names.

## Who approves, and who cannot

The person who builds a change must not be the sole approver and deployer of it.
A change approved by its own author records an authorisation that never
happened: there was no second judgement, only a signature the requester wrote
for themselves. The issue is segregation of duties, not which line reviews: a
second reviewer in the same first line is fine; the author signing for themselves
is not.

Segregation here is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a control that
catches a bad or malicious change before release and one that only documents it
afterwards. The approver need not be senior; they need to be someone other than
the author, with enough context to refuse.

## The three change types

ITIL 4 and many control frameworks sort changes into three paths, and the point
of the split is to match the weight of the process to the risk of the change
rather than force everything through one gate.

| Type | Path | Approval | Review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Assessed and approved through the standard route | Before release, by someone other than the author | At release |
| Standard | Pre-authorised, low-risk and repeatable | Granted once, to the change type | On the pre-authorisation, periodically |
| Emergency | Expedited for genuine urgency | Compressed, by an emergency approver | Retrospective, and mandatory |

The boundaries (what counts as low-risk, how fast an emergency has to be) are
the organisation's own to set; the three-way split is the part that travels. The
emergency path exists for real incidents where waiting for normal approval would
do more harm than the change. Authorisation is compressed (an emergency approver
signs quickly), not removed; deferring approval to a retrospective review is an
exceptional contingency, not the design. It is also the one most often abused,
because it is the fastest route through the gate.

## Where it fails

Four failure modes account for most change-management findings, and a tester who
knows them looks for them first.

**The emergency back door.** When a large share of changes come through the
emergency path, the path has stopped being for emergencies. It has become the
route the organisation uses to skip approval, and a rate (emergency changes as
a proportion of all changes) is the single most revealing number in the
process.

**Approver equals requester.** The record shows an approval, but the approver
and the requester are the same person, or the same shared account. There is a
signature and no authorisation. This is the failure segregation exists to
prevent, and it survives in programmes that check the box for "approved" without
checking who approved.

**"Tested" with no test.** The change record asserts the change was tested and
carries nothing that shows a test ran: no result, no environment, no output. An
assertion of testing is not evidence of testing, and an auditor treats the two
very differently.

**Standing approvals.** A blanket or standing approval authorises a category of
change rather than a change, so everything inside the category ships without a
fresh decision. Standard changes are the bounded, reviewed version of this; a
standing approval that is really an open door is not.

Each of these is a control deviation before anyone has named it. When a tester
samples releases and finds one that reached production with no approval on file,
or an approval signed by its own author, that is the deviation the report
records, and the emergency queue is a place testers commonly look first.

:::def term="Evidence" ref="4.2"
A dated record produced by the operation of a control, sufficient for a reviewer
who was not present to conclude that the control ran. A change record is
evidence in exactly this sense, which is why "tested: yes" with nothing behind
it fails.
:::

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is a change management control?
It is the control that a change to production is authorised, tested and documented before it is released, so that no change reaches a live system without a record of who requested it, who approved it, what was tested and when it went out. ISO/IEC 27001's Annex A control 8.32 and the Trust Services criterion CC8.1 both frame it this way. The control is operating when that record exists for every change and the approver is never the author.

### What is an emergency change?
An emergency change is one released through an expedited path because genuine urgency (an active incident, a live outage) makes waiting for normal approval more harmful than the change itself. Authorisation is expedited, compressed to an emergency approver, and only exceptionally deferred to a mandatory retrospective review. The path is legitimate and necessary; it is also the one most often abused, so the proportion of changes that use it is worth watching.

### What evidence does an auditor want for change management?
The change record: the request, the approval by someone other than the requester, the test result and the deployment, each attributable to a named person and dated by the system. For a sample of changes across the whole period, not the best one. A change with a missing approval, an approval by its own author or a "tested" flag with no test behind it is recorded as a deviation.
:::

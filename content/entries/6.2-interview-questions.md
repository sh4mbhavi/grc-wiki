:::lede
Every one of these questions has a textbook answer and a real answer. The
textbook answer gets you to the next question; the real answer gets you the job.
:::

What interviewers are testing, almost without exception, is whether you have
held the artefact or only read about it. Answers that name a specific instance
do that; answers that recite a definition do not.

## The eight

:::faq
### What is the difference between GRC and internal audit?

The accountability line, not the task list. "GRC reports to management and owns
the outcome; internal audit reports to the audit committee and owns the
opinion." Everything about tools and frameworks follows from it.

The follow-up is usually about independence, and the answer they are listening
for is that it is structural rather than personal — an auditor who helped fix
the gap has lost the opinion they were hired to give, however honest they are.
See [1.4](/foundations/grc-vs-internal-audit/).

### Appetite or tolerance — what is the difference?

Appetite is how much risk the organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of
its objectives, set annually by the board, usually by category. Tolerance is the
acceptable variation around one specific objective, and it is a number you can
cross.

The good answer adds when the distinction bites: at the moment somebody has to
decide whether a specific risk gets treated or accepted. See
[3.2](/risk/appetite-vs-tolerance/).

### Walk me through a risk you assessed.

The one that matters. Pick a real one, and include what went wrong.

How it was raised. How you scored it, against what documented scale. Who owned
it — a person. What treatment was chosen and who had the authority to approve
it. What evidence closed it. Then the honest part: what you would do
differently, which is where the interview stops being a test.

### How do you handle a control owner who will not give you evidence?

They are testing whether you escalate straight away, which is the wrong answer.

Find out why first — usually the request is ambiguous, the system cannot produce
what you asked for, or they do not know they own the control. Then make the
request specific and easy. Escalation is the third move, not the first, and when
it comes it goes with a record of the two attempts before it.

### ISO 27001 or SOC 2?

Answer with a question: who is asking for it. North American technology buyers
ask for SOC 2; European and enterprise procurement asks for ISO. If both,
sequence rather than choose.

Candidates who answer with a preference are describing a document they have read.
Candidates who answer with the buyer are describing work they have done. See
[5.1](/frameworks/iso-27001-vs-soc-2/).

### Is GRC a technical job?

No, but it is a literate one. You need to read a system diagram and challenge an
engineer's answer; you do not need to configure the system.

Worth adding the failure mode, because it shows self-awareness: the risk is not
being insufficiently technical, it is accepting an answer you did not understand
because asking again felt awkward.

### How would you build a programme from nothing?

Register first, even a rough one, because the argument about the scale is easier
with real entries in front of it. Then the policy set, then the control
inventory with its mapping, then the evidence habit.

Starting with policy is the intuitive answer and the slow one — you end up
writing rules for risks you have not established you carry. Saying so
demonstrates you have thought about order rather than listing artefacts.

### Where do you see the function failing?

An invitation, and most candidates waste it on something generic about tone at
the top.

Better: name a failure mode you have seen. Registers where nothing has moved in
two quarters. Treatment columns full of "monitor". Evidence collected in the
four weeks before fieldwork. Each of those is specific, checkable, and shows you
know what a working function looks like because you know what a broken one looks
like.
:::

## What to ask them

Two questions worth more than a dozen polite ones.

*How do you know this function is working?* Coverage percentages describe a
function that reports on itself; decisions made and surprises avoided describe
one that is used.

*What was the last finding you disagreed with, and what happened?* The answer
tells you whether the function has standing, or whether it writes what it is
asked to write.

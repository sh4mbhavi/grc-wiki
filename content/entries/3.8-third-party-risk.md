:::lede
Third-party risk management is identifying, **tiering and monitoring the risk
that suppliers and vendors carry into the organisation** — because a control you
depend on is now operated by someone you do not manage.
:::

Treat "vendor", "supplier" and "third party" as one population here. The words
sort procurement paperwork; the risk they carry is the same, and so is the
technique for handling it. Every supplier operates a service or control the
organisation has chosen to run outside its own walls, and the register at
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/)
carries the consequences when one fails.

## Tiering: not every supplier gets the same scrutiny

The first move is to sort the population by what each supplier could actually
hurt — access to production data, position inside a continuity RTO, regulatory
exposure — and never by what it costs. Depth of due diligence then scales with
the tier, so effort lands where the exposure is.

| Tier | What the supplier can hurt | Due-diligence depth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Holds production or customer data, or sits inside a recovery time objective | Full assessment: security review, financial stability, evidence-backed or on-site audit, named executive sponsor |
| Important | Regulatory exposure, or access to limited personal data | Questionnaire with evidence sampled, contractual controls verified before signing |
| Routine | No sensitive data, readily replaced | Standard terms, a register entry and light reassessment by exception |

A cheap supplier with a live connection to production belongs in the top tier. A
large invoice for stationery does not. Spend is the wrong axis.

## The lifecycle

TPRM is not an onboarding gate. It runs from before the contract to after it, and
each stage leaves evidence the next one depends on.

:::terms
Onboarding due diligence :: The assessment done before signing, scaled to tier — security posture, financial stability and regulatory standing, judged on the evidence behind the answers rather than the answers alone.
Contractual controls :: The rights that must survive the honeymoon: a right to audit, a breach-notification window measured in hours and limits on which sub-processors may be used and what data they may touch.
Ongoing monitoring :: Reassessment on a cadence set by tier, plus watching for the events that change the tier — a breach, a change of control, a service moved offshore.
Offboarding :: Access revoked, credentials killed and data returned or destroyed with evidence. The step skipped most often, and the first one an auditor checks.
:::

:::def term="Fourth-party risk" ref="3.1"
The risk that arrives through your supplier's own suppliers — the parties you have no contract with and cannot assess directly. It is managed indirectly, by requiring each critical third party to control and disclose its own critical dependencies.
:::

## Where TPRM fails

**Due diligence done once.** The assessment is completed at onboarding, filed and
never repeated. The supplier's risk is frozen at the one moment it was lowest —
before it had access to anything.

**Tiering by contract value.** The largest invoice gets the deepest review while a
cheap supplier with production access is waved through. That is usually where the
incident comes from.

**A questionnaire nobody reads.** Two hundred answered questions, no evidence
sampled, no follow-up on the "no" that should have stopped the deal. A completed
questionnaire is not assurance; a verified one is.

**Concentration risk.** Ten services behind one provider, each passed on its own
and nobody added them up. Aggregate exposure is the risk function's job — see
[3.0](/risk/what-the-risk-function-produces/) — not the procurement queue's.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is the difference between vendor risk and third-party risk?
Little worth managing to separately. "Vendor" usually names a supplier paid for a product or service; "third party" is the wider set that also includes partners, agents, resellers and anyone else operating a control on your behalf. The techniques — tiering, due diligence, monitoring, offboarding — are identical, so both are handled as one population. The distinction that does matter is risk versus compliance, covered at [1.9](/foundations/risk-vs-compliance/).

### What is fourth-party risk?
The risk from your suppliers' own suppliers. A cloud host depends on a data-centre operator; a payroll processor depends on a hosting provider you have never named. You hold no contract with them and cannot assess them directly, so the control is contractual: require each critical third party to manage, and disclose, its own critical dependencies.

### How often should a third party be reassessed?
On a cadence set by tier, not a single calendar rule. Critical suppliers annually at least, and immediately on a trigger — a breach, a change of control, a service relocated. Routine suppliers can run on a longer cycle or by exception. A flat "every three years for everyone" repeats the tiering-by-spend mistake: it decouples effort from risk.
:::

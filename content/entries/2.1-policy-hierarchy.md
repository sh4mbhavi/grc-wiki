:::lede
Three levels, and each answers a different question. The policy says *what is
required*. The standard says *how much is enough*. The procedure says *how to
do it*.
:::

Collapsing them is the standard failure. An organisation writes one
forty-page "Information Security Policy" containing password lengths and
step-by-step joiner instructions, then discovers it must go back to the board
every time a tool changes.

## The three levels

| Level | Answers | Changes | Approved by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Policy | What is required, and why | Rarely — annually at most | Board or delegated committee |
| Standard | How much is enough, measurably | When the threat or tooling moves | Function head |
| Procedure | How the work is done, step by step | Whenever the tool changes | Process owner |

The approval column is the reason the split exists. Different levels change at
different speeds and warrant different levels of scrutiny; binding them into one
document forces the slowest approval path onto the fastest-moving content.

## A worked example

**Policy:** access to systems holding customer data is granted on the principle
of least privilege and reviewed at defined intervals.

**Standard:** privileged access is reviewed quarterly; standard access
semi-annually; reviews are completed within 15 working days of issue.

**Procedure:** export the entitlement report from the identity provider, filter
to the in-scope groups, distribute to the named owners, track responses in the
review tracker, revoke on instruction, attach the completed tracker to the
control record.

Note what moves where. Replace the identity provider and only the procedure
changes. Tighten the cadence to monthly and only the standard changes. The
policy is untouched by either.

:::def term="Least privilege" ref="4.1"
The principle that an identity holds only the access required for its current
duties, for only as long as those duties last.
:::

## The tests

A policy that names a product is a standard. A standard with no number in it is a
policy. A procedure that requires judgement to follow is a standard pretending.

One more, useful when reviewing someone else's document set: pick any rule and
ask who approved it and when. If nobody can answer for a rule the organisation is
enforcing against employees, the hierarchy has failed regardless of how many
levels it has on paper.

:::lede
The three lines model describes how accountability for risk is distributed:
the people doing the work own it, a second group provides expertise and
challenge, and a third gives an independent opinion to the governing body.
:::

The Institute of Internal Auditors published the current version in 2020,
replacing a 2013 paper that used the phrase *three lines of defence*. The
rewrite was not cosmetic. "Defence" implied risk was something arriving from
outside to be repelled, and implied the lines were sequential barriers. Both
readings produced organisations where the first line assumed the second would
catch its mistakes.

## The three lines

:::terms
First line :: The people who deliver the product or service. They own the risks their activity creates and operate the controls inside it. Not a junior line — most control operation happens here.
Second line :: Specialist functions providing expertise, frameworks, challenge and monitoring. Risk, compliance, and most GRC roles sit here. It supports the first line and reports to management.
Third line :: Internal audit. Independent assurance to the governing body on the effectiveness of everything the first two lines do. It reports to the audit committee, not to management.
:::

The governing body sits above all three, and management sits between the body
and the first two lines. External assurance providers — a certification body, a
SOC 2 auditor, a regulator — sit outside the model entirely.

## What the 2020 revision changed

Three things worth knowing if you were taught the older version.

**Collaboration replaced separation.** The 2013 framing encouraged lines to
operate independently of each other. The 2020 model states explicitly that the
lines should collaborate, with independence required only for the third.

**The lines are roles, not teams.** A person can hold first-line and second-line
responsibilities in different parts of their job. The model constrains the
combination — you cannot independently assure your own work — but does not
require three org charts.

**Risk is upside as well as downside.** The current model frames the lines around
achieving objectives, not only avoiding harm.

## Where GRC sits

Second line, in almost every case. GRC provides the frameworks the first line
operates within, monitors whether they are being followed, and escalates when
they are not.

The common exception: in a small organisation, GRC frequently ends up operating
first-line controls too — running the access review rather than checking it ran.
That is a resourcing reality rather than a model violation, but it needs to be
visible, because it removes the challenge the second line exists to provide.

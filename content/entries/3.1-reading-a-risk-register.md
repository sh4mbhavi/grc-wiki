:::lede
A risk register is the list of things that could stop the organisation
achieving what it set out to do, each with a size, a named owner, and a
decision about what happens next.
:::

It is the most-read artefact in the discipline and the most frequently
abandoned. Both facts have the same cause: a register is cheap to start and
expensive to keep honest.

## The columns that carry weight

:::terms
Risk statement :: A cause, an event and a consequence in one sentence. "Supplier X fails, our checkout is unavailable, we lose transactions and breach the SLA." Not a topic — "supply chain" is not a risk.
Inherent score :: How large it would be with no controls in place. Argued over more than it deserves; useful mainly for showing how much the controls are earning.
Controls :: What is currently in place, mapped to the control inventory rather than described freehand.
Residual score :: The size after those controls. This is the number the appetite is applied to.
Owner :: One named person who can authorise the treatment. Not a team, not a function.
Treatment :: Accept, mitigate, transfer or avoid — with a date and a target score if it is anything but accept.
Review date :: When somebody last looked, and when they will look next.
:::

Registers commonly carry more columns than this. They rarely carry fewer and
remain usable.

## The scoring, and what it is for

Likelihood times impact, on whatever scale the organisation has documented —
usually 5 × 5, giving scores from 1 to 25. See [1.1](/foundations/what-grc-is/)
for the matrix itself.

Precision is not the goal. Two competent assessors will land in adjacent cells,
and that is fine. What matters is that the bands are defined in writing, that a
score can be traced to the criteria that produced it, and that the same criteria
were applied last quarter. A register whose scale lives in one person's head
produces numbers that evaporate when they leave.

:::def term="Residual risk" ref="3.2"
The level of risk remaining after existing controls are taken into account. The
number appetite is tested against.
:::

## Four signs a register has stopped working

**Nothing has moved in two quarters.** Scores that never change mean nobody is
reassessing them. Real registers churn.

**Every owner is a team.** "Owned by: Engineering" means owned by nobody. When
the treatment slips, there is no one to ask.

**The treatment column reads "monitor".** Monitoring is not a treatment; it is
the absence of one, written to avoid the accept decision that someone would have
to sign.

**Closed risks disappear.** A risk that was accepted and later closed should
still be visible with its history. A register that only shows what is open cannot
answer "did we know about this?", which is the question asked after an incident.

## What to do when handed one cold

Sort by residual score descending. Read the top ten statements and check each is
a sentence with a consequence in it. Look at the review dates. Then find the
appetite statement and check the top ten are actually above it — if they are
not, either the appetite is set too high to be meaningful, or the register is
only recording what was comfortable to write down.

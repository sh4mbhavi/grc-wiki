:::lede
Most arguments inside a GRC function are vocabulary arguments wearing a
disguise. These are the words the rest of the reference uses, in the sense the
standards use them.
:::

Where a term has a precise definition in a standard, that definition is given
and cited. Where practice has drifted from the standard — and it has, in several
places — the drift is named rather than smoothed over.

## The core fourteen

:::terms
Risk :: The effect of uncertainty on objectives. Note the direction: a risk is defined against something you are trying to achieve, which is why a register with no objectives behind it drifts into a list of things people dislike.
Issue :: A risk that has occurred. The distinction is operational: risks are managed on a review cycle, issues are managed to a closure date.
Threat :: The thing that could cause the event, not the risk itself. "Ransomware" is a threat; "ransomware encrypts the order database and we cannot ship for four days" is a risk.
Control :: A measure that modifies risk. Includes process, policy and practice, not only technology — and a control that is not operating is not a control.
Obligation :: A requirement that binds the organisation, from law, regulation, contract or a standard it has committed to. The source is part of the obligation; one with no citation cannot be tested.
Evidence :: A dated record produced by the operation of a control, sufficient for a reviewer who was not present.
Assurance :: A conclusion about whether something works, given by someone with the standing to give it. First party from management, second party from a customer, third party from an independent auditor.
Appetite :: The amount of risk the organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.
Tolerance :: The acceptable variation around a specific objective. Distinct from appetite, and confused with it constantly.
Inherent risk :: The level before controls are taken into account. Useful mainly for showing what the controls are earning.
Residual risk :: The level after existing controls. This is the number appetite is applied to.
Treatment :: What is being done about a risk — accept, mitigate, transfer or avoid.
Owner :: One named person with the authority to decide. Not a team, not a function, not a role that is currently vacant.
Scope :: What sits inside the boundary of a certification, an assessment or a register — and by implication what sits outside it, and is therefore unexamined.
:::

## Three that are used loosely

**Compliant.** Nothing is compliant in the abstract. Something is compliant
*with* a named obligation, at a point in time, on the evidence available. A
vendor that says its product is "GDPR compliant" has said nothing testable.

**Framework.** Used for four different things: a certifiable standard
(ISO 27001), a descriptive model (NIST CSF 2.0), an attestation criteria set
(SOC 2) and an internal control model (COSO ERM). The difference determines what
you can do with the output, which is why it is worth keeping straight.

**Maturity.** A statement about how consistently a process runs, not about how
good it is. A consistently bad process scores well on maturity — which is why a
maturity score alone never answers "are we safe".

:::def term="Risk owner" ref="3.1"
The one named person accountable for a risk staying inside appetite, with the
authority to approve its treatment. Distinct from the control owner, who
operates the measure.
:::

## Why the precision earns its keep

An auditor's first move on a register is to read three risk statements and check
they are risks rather than threats or issues. It takes ninety seconds and tells
them how much of the rest to trust. The same is true of a control inventory full
of aspirations, or an obligation register with no citations.

Getting the words right is not pedantry. It is the cheapest available signal
that the function knows what it is doing.

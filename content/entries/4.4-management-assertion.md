:::lede
In an attestation engagement such as SOC 2, management provides a written
**assertion**: its own statement that the system is described fairly and the
controls were suitably designed. The practitioner then opines on that assertion —
the words are management's, not the auditor's.
:::

For a Type II the assertion reaches further: it states that those controls
operated effectively across the whole reporting period, not merely that they were
well designed on one day. The report is built on top of this statement. The
practitioner's opinion is a conclusion about management's assertion, so a weak or
overstated assertion is management's exposure and does not transfer to the firm
that examined it.

## The assertion is management's, the opinion is the auditor's

These are two separate statements by two separate parties, and running them
together is the most common misreading of a SOC 2 report. The assertion comes
first: management describes the system and states that the description is fair and
the controls suitably designed. The opinion is written on top of it — the
practitioner tests, then concludes on whether management's assertion is fairly
stated. Under SSAE 18, AT-C section 205, the examination is of a subject matter
for which the responsible party has made an assertion; without that assertion
there is nothing for the practitioner to report on.

| Aspect | The assertion | The opinion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written by | Management | The practitioner, a licensed CPA firm |
| States | The description is fair and controls were suitably designed | Whether that assertion is fairly stated, on the evidence tested |
| Order | Comes first | Built on the assertion |
| Carries the exposure | Management owns it | The firm answers for its own testing |

## What the assertion covers

:::terms
Fair presentation :: The system description matches the system that actually ran — its boundaries, the services in scope and the controls in place. Overstate the boundary and the assertion is signed over a system that does not exist.
Suitability of design :: The controls, as designed, would meet the applicable Trust Services Criteria if they operated as described. This, together with fair presentation, is the whole of a Type I.
Operating effectiveness :: For a Type II, that the controls operated effectively throughout the stated period. This is the part the practitioner tests against a sample.
The criteria :: Which criteria each half is measured against. The description is assessed for fair presentation against the description criteria (DC section 200); the controls are assessed against the Trust Services Criteria. Naming both keeps the report from being read against the wrong bar.
:::

:::def term="Type I and Type II" ref="5.1"
A Type I assertion covers the fair presentation of the system description and the
suitability of control design at a point in time; a Type II adds that those
controls operated effectively across a period. The same Type I / Type II split is
used for SOC 1 reports.
:::

## Where ISO 27001 puts the same accountability

ISO/IEC 27001 has no direct equivalent. Certification produces a certificate from
an accredited body, not an opinion on a statement management signed. The
accountability lives in different machinery: clause 9.3 requires top management to
review the information security management system at planned intervals, and the
Statement of Applicability records which controls apply and the justification for
including or excluding each. Both put a management decision on record. Neither is
a signed assertion a practitioner then
opines on, so an ISO certificate should never be described as resting on a
management assertion.

## How an assertion fails

**Scope overstated.** The description claims systems, locations or services the
controls do not actually cover, and management signs it anyway. When the sample
reaches the part that was never really in scope, the gap is management's — it
asserted something untrue.

**Effectiveness the evidence cannot support.** Management asserts that controls
operated throughout the period when the evidence covers only part of it. A Type II
assertion is that the controls operated effectively across the whole period, in
all material respects — not a claim resting only on the instances that happened to
be captured.

**Treated as boilerplate.** The assertion is read as text the auditor drafted and
management merely countersigned. It is the opposite: the statement management owns
and the document the whole report is built to opine on. A programme that lets the
firm word its assertion has misunderstood who is exposed.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is the difference between the management assertion and the auditor's opinion?

The assertion is management's statement; the opinion is the practitioner's
conclusion about that statement. Management writes the assertion first — that the
system is described fairly and the controls were suitably designed, and for a
Type II operated effectively over the period. The CPA firm then tests and issues
an opinion on whether the assertion is fairly stated. One is the claim, the other
is the verdict on the claim, and they are written by different parties.

### Who signs the management assertion?

A member of management with authority over the system in scope — commonly an
executive such as the chief executive, chief technology officer or head of
security, depending on who owns the system description. It is signed by the
organisation being examined, never by the audit firm. The signature is the point:
it records that management, not the practitioner, stands behind the description
and the controls.

### Is there a management assertion in an ISO 27001 audit?

Not in the SOC 2 sense. ISO/IEC 27001 certification produces a certificate from an
accredited body rather than an opinion on a signed statement. The nearest
accountability sits in the management review required by clause 9.3 and in the
Statement of Applicability, both of which record management decisions — but
neither is a written assertion the auditor opines on.
:::

:::lede
**Business continuity** is keeping the organisation's critical activities
running, or restoring them within an acceptable time, when a disruption hits.
Disaster recovery is the subset of that work concerned only with IT systems.
:::

The two terms are used as synonyms and are not. Disaster recovery brings the
technology back. Business continuity keeps the business function alive, by
technology or by anything else that works - a manual process, an alternate
site, staff held in reserve. One is a component of the other.

## The line between continuity and disaster recovery

| Dimension | Business continuity | Disaster recovery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | The critical business activity | The IT systems beneath it |
| The question it answers | How does the function keep running? | How do the systems come back? |
| Owns | The business line that runs the activity | IT or infrastructure |
| Trigger | Any disruption to the activity | Loss or failure of technology |
| Output | Workarounds, alternate arrangements, roles | Restored systems, recovered data |

A disruption need not be technological to matter. A picket line, a burst pipe, a
supplier failing, a key team unreachable - none of these is a disaster recovery
event, and all of them stop the activity. Continuity has to cover the cases that
have no server in them at all.

## What a continuity plan contains

A plan is not a policy statement. It is the specific set of things that let a
named activity survive, written so that someone under pressure can follow it.

:::terms
Critical activities :: The processes the organisation cannot be without for long, taken from the business impact analysis rather than guessed at.
Recovery time objective :: The time within which each activity must be back, derived in the BIA at [3.7](/risk/business-impact-analysis/), not asserted by the plan.
Dependencies :: What the activity actually needs to run - people, suppliers, systems and premises. A plan that lists only systems has missed most of them.
Alternate arrangements :: The workaround. How the activity runs while its normal means are gone, and who is trained to run it that way.
Invocation and roles :: Who decides the plan is live, who does what once it is and who deputises when the first name is unreachable.
Communications :: How staff, customers and regulators are told, with contact routes that do not depend on the failed system.
:::

## What a recovery time objective forces

:::def term="Recovery time objective" ref="3.7"
The target time within which a critical activity must be restored after a
disruption, set shorter than the point at which the harm becomes unacceptable.
Derived in the business impact analysis, not chosen by IT.
:::

An RTO of four hours on order processing is not a statement about servers. It
forces a set of arrangements to be standing by before the disruption: staff who
can be reached and trained on the workaround, an alternate way to take orders
that does not route through the failed system, and suppliers who have agreed to
the same window. If any of those takes six hours to stand up, the four-hour RTO
is fiction. The number is only as real as the slowest dependency behind it.

## Where continuity plans fail

**Never exercised.** A plan that has not been walked through is an assertion, not
a capability. The first invocation is not the moment to discover the alternate
site has no network drops or the workaround needs a licence nobody bought.

**RTOs asserted with no BIA behind them.** A recovery time picked to look
reassuring, rather than derived from what the activity actually needs, sets a
target the arrangements were never built to hit.

**A plan written for the building, not the process.** Evacuation and premises
cover is not continuity. If the plan says where people go but not how the work
continues once they are there, the activity still stops.

**Contact lists that rot.** People move, numbers change, deputies leave. A list
maintained once at authoring is wrong within a year, and it is wrong at exactly
the moment it is needed.

:::faq
### Is business continuity the same as disaster recovery?
No. Disaster recovery restores technology - systems, data, connectivity.
Business continuity keeps the critical activity running by whatever means, which
may be a manual workaround, an alternate site or standby staff that never touch
the failed system. Disaster recovery is one input to continuity, not a synonym
for it.

### How often should a continuity plan be tested?
On planned intervals and again after any material change to the process, its
people or its dependencies - ISO 22301 frames the cadence that way rather than as
a fixed figure, though annual is the common floor in practice. The test need not
be a full invocation each time; a
tabletop walkthrough of one plausible scenario exposes most of the gaps that
matter, at a fraction of the cost.

### Who owns business continuity?
The business line that runs the activity owns its continuity, because only it can
authorise the workaround and carry it out. A central function may coordinate the
programme, set the method and hold the plans, but it cannot own the recovery of
a process it does not run.
:::

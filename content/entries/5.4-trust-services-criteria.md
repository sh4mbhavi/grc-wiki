:::lede
The **Trust Services Criteria** are the AICPA control criteria a SOC 2 examination is measured against, grouped into five categories — Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality and Privacy — of which only Security is mandatory. The other four are included when they are relevant to the service.
:::

Which categories a report covers is a scoping decision, not a fixed list, and it is one of the biggest levers on what a SOC 2 costs and how long it takes. A report can be scoped to Security alone and be a complete, valid SOC 2 report. Adding a category adds criteria, adds the controls that meet them and adds the evidence an auditor must test.

## The five categories

Before the 2017 revision these were called the trust services principles; the criteria themselves are published by the AICPA as TSP section 100.

| Category | Mandatory? | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security | Yes — the common criteria | Systems and information are protected against unauthorised access, unauthorised disclosure and damage. Present in every SOC 2 report. |
| Availability | Optional | The system is available for operation and use as committed or agreed. |
| Processing Integrity | Optional | System processing is complete, valid, accurate, timely and authorised. |
| Confidentiality | Optional | Information designated as confidential is protected as committed. |
| Privacy | Optional | Personal information is collected, used, retained, disclosed and disposed of in line with the entity's own privacy notice. |

Security is the only category that must be in scope, and it is met by the **common criteria** — the CC series, which every SOC 2 report includes. The other four are selected by relevance to the service and to the commitments the entity has actually made to its customers. Most reports are Security only, or Security with Availability and often Confidentiality. Adding Privacy is often the largest single increase in scope, because it brings in a whole additional body of criteria about personal information.

A common misconception is that a SOC 2 report covers all five categories. It does not. Only Security is mandatory; a category appears only when the entity chooses it, and a report scoped to Security alone is not a lesser or partial SOC 2. Note also that Confidentiality and Privacy are different jobs: Confidentiality protects any information the entity has agreed to treat as confidential, whatever its subject; Privacy is specifically about personal information and the entity's handling of it.

## Inside the common criteria

The common criteria are called common because they apply to every engagement regardless of which categories are in scope, and because they constitute the whole of the Security category on their own. In the 2017 criteria, revised 2022, they run from CC1 to CC9.

The first five families, CC1 to CC5, are built directly on the five components of the 2013 COSO Internal Control – Integrated Framework: the control environment (CC1), communication and information (CC2), risk assessment (CC3), monitoring activities (CC4) and control activities (CC5). CC6 to CC9 are additional criteria the AICPA layered on for information security specifically: logical and physical access controls (CC6); system operations (CC7); change management (CC8) and risk mitigation (CC9).

Each optional category then adds a short supplemental series on top of the common criteria, rather than replacing anything.

:::terms
Availability (A1) :: A single supplemental series covering capacity, environmental protections and backup, recovery and incident handling that keep the system available as committed.
Processing Integrity (PI1) :: Criteria that inputs, processing and outputs are complete, valid, accurate, timely and authorised. Most relevant where the completeness or accuracy of processing is itself part of the service commitment.
Confidentiality (C1) :: Criteria that information the entity has agreed to treat as confidential is protected through its lifecycle and disposed of when no longer needed.
Privacy (P1–P8) :: Eight supplemental groupings — notice and communication; choice and consent; collection; use, retention and disposal; access; disclosure and notification; quality; and monitoring and enforcement. Controls are evaluated against these criteria in light of the entity's privacy commitments and notice, not as a legal-compliance opinion on any privacy law.
:::

## Two sets of criteria, two jobs

The Trust Services Criteria are not the only criteria a SOC 2 report is written against, and confusing them with the other set is a genuine misreading. The Trust Services Criteria are what the controls are evaluated against — whether the controls that meet Security and any other category in scope are suitably designed and, in a Type 2 report, operating effectively. The description criteria (DC section 200) govern something different: whether the system description management writes is presented fairly. One measures the controls; the other measures the account of the system those controls sit in. They are separate documents doing separate jobs.

:::def term="Description criteria" ref="4.4"
The criteria (DC section 200) for fair presentation of the system description in a SOC 2 report — its boundaries, the services in scope and the controls in place. The system description is assessed against these, while the controls are assessed against the Trust Services Criteria; the [management assertion](/compliance/management-assertion/) addresses both.
:::

## Choosing categories

Category selection is the main lever on scope and cost, so choose by two things: what the entity genuinely commits to in its customer contracts and what its buyers are actually asking for. If customers ask for uptime commitments, Availability earns its place. If the service holds confidential customer data under contract, Confidentiality does. If a report is being read alongside an [ISO 27001 certificate](/frameworks/iso-27001-vs-soc-2/), the sensible sequence is to scope the categories the buyer names rather than every category available.

Privacy is the category most often added for the wrong reason. It signals maturity, so it gets bolted on — but it demands a real privacy programme measured against a published notice, and an auditor who finds no such programme will report the gap. Over-scoping does not strengthen a report; it enlarges the surface on which it can fall short.

## Where it goes wrong

**"SOC 2 covers all five categories."** It does not. Only Security is mandatory, and the other four appear only when the entity selects them. A report scoped to Security, or to Security and Availability, is a complete SOC 2 report — reading the absence of Privacy or Processing Integrity as a deficiency is a mistake buyers and sellers both make.

**Privacy read as a legal-compliance opinion.** The Privacy category tests handling of personal information against the entity's own privacy notice and commitments. It is not an opinion on GDPR, on any national privacy law or on the adequacy of the notice itself. A SOC 2 Privacy report does not certify legal compliance, and it should never be sold or read as if it did.

**Bolting on a category with no real programme.** Adding Privacy or Processing Integrity to look thorough, without the controls those criteria require, invites a qualified opinion or an exception in the very area meant to impress. Categories are commitments the report then has to prove.

**Confusing the two sets of criteria.** Judging the system description against the Trust Services Criteria, or the controls against the description criteria, measures each thing against the wrong bar. Controls answer to the Trust Services Criteria; the fairness of the description answers to DC section 200.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is Privacy required in a SOC 2 report?

No. Security is the only mandatory category. Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality and Privacy are each optional and included only when relevant to the service and to the commitments the entity has made. Privacy is a substantial addition, because it brings its own supplemental criteria (P1 to P8) about personal information, so it is added when there is a real privacy programme to report on, not by default.

### Does SOC 2 cover all five Trust Services Criteria?

Not unless the entity scopes all five in. A SOC 2 report always includes Security, the common criteria present in every report, and then whichever of the other four categories the entity has selected. Most reports are Security only, or Security with Availability and Confidentiality. Treating every SOC 2 as a five-category report misreads what has actually been examined.

### What is the difference between the Trust Services Criteria and the description criteria?

The Trust Services Criteria (TSP section 100) are what the controls are tested against. The description criteria (DC section 200) are what the system description is tested against for fair presentation. They are different documents doing different jobs in the same report: one governs the controls, the other governs the account of the system the controls operate in.
:::

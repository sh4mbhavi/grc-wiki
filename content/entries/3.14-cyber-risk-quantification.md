:::lede
Cyber risk quantification states a specific risk as a **probable financial loss
over a year, with a probability attached** - for example a 10% chance of losing
more than £4m to a customer-data breach - so it can be weighed against the cost
of fixing it and against the appetite the board set.
:::

It exists because the 5×5 heat map cannot answer the question a board actually
asks. "Amber" does not tell you whether to spend £200k on a control, and two
"high" risks on the same grid may differ by three orders of magnitude in real
exposure. Quantification replaces the colour with a distribution of outcomes in
the currency the rest of the business already plans in.

The move is not from *imprecise* to *precise*. It is from an ordinal label that
cannot be added, compared or budgeted against to a range that can. A quantified
estimate of "£1m to £20m, most likely £6m" is more useful than "high" even
though it is still an estimate.

## FAIR, the common method

Most programmes use FAIR - Factor Analysis of Information Risk, standardised by
The Open Group. FAIR decomposes a risk into parts small enough to estimate, then
recombines them by simulation.

:::terms
Loss Event Frequency :: How often the loss event happens in a year. Split into how often a threat acts against the asset and how often that action gets through the controls.
Loss Magnitude :: What one event costs. Primary loss is the direct hit - response, replacement, lost revenue during downtime. Secondary loss is the reaction of others - fines, legal claims, customers leaving.
Distribution, not point :: Each factor is entered as a range with a confidence, not a single number, because that is the honest shape of what an analyst knows.
Monte Carlo :: The model samples every range thousands of times and produces a curve of annual loss, not one figure.
:::

The headline output is a **loss exceedance curve**: for every loss amount, the
probability of exceeding it in a year. The board reads one line off it - "10%
chance of losing more than £4m" - and compares that to the appetite statement
and to the price of the control that would move the curve.

:::def term="Annualised loss expectancy" ref="3.12"
The older single-number form: expected loss frequency multiplied by expected
loss per event, giving one currency figure per year. Easy to compute, but it
hides the tail - the rare, ruinous outcome that a curve shows and an average
buries.
:::

## Where it earns its place, and where it does not

Quantify the risks where the number changes a decision: a control investment
that needs a business case, a cyber-insurance limit that has to be set, a small
number of enterprise-level exposures the board tracks by name. For those, a
defensible range beats a colour every time.

Do not quantify the whole register. Most entries are operational, are treated
because policy or a framework requires it, and would consume analyst weeks to
model without changing what anyone does. The heat map at
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/) is still the right tool for the routine
majority; quantification is a scalpel for the few that matter most.

## Where cyber risk quantification fails

**False precision.** A curve drawn to the pound from inputs the analyst guessed
in five minutes. The output inherits the confidence of its weakest input, and
presenting it without the assumptions invites a decision it cannot support.

**Uncalibrated estimators.** People are reliably overconfident: their "90%
confident" ranges contain the true value far less than 90% of the time.
Estimates from anyone who has not done calibration training are the largest
error source in the model, larger than the choice of method.

**Modelling everything.** The programme tries to quantify 300 register lines,
stalls after 12, and the effort is written off. Scope it to the handful of risks
where a number changes a decision.

**A number with no owner.** The analysis is run once by a consultant, delivered
as a slide, and never refreshed when the environment changes. A quantified risk
needs the same owner and review cadence as any other register entry.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is cyber risk quantification?

Expressing a specific cyber risk as a probable financial loss with a probability
- such as a 15% annual chance of a loss above £2m - instead of a qualitative
label like "high". It lets a risk be compared against the cost of treating it,
against an insurance limit and against a stated appetite, using the same units
the business plans in.

### What is the FAIR model?

Factor Analysis of Information Risk: a standard method, published by The Open
Group, that breaks a risk into loss event frequency and loss magnitude,
estimates each as a range, and runs a Monte Carlo simulation to produce a curve
of annual loss. It is the most widely used quantification method in security
risk.

### Is cyber risk quantification better than a heat map?

For a small number of board-level exposures, yes - a money range can be
budgeted against and a colour cannot. For the routine majority of a register it
is not worth the effort, and a well-run 5×5 remains the practical tool. Most
mature programmes use both.

### Do you need a lot of data to quantify cyber risk?

No. The method is built for sparse data: inputs are calibrated ranges that
reflect uncertainty honestly, not measured frequencies. Industry loss datasets
help, but the larger determinant of quality is whether the people giving
estimates have been trained to be calibrated.
:::

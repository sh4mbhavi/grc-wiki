:::lede
**System and Organization Controls (SOC)** reports are a suite of attestation reports defined by the AICPA and issued by a licensed CPA firm on a service organisation's controls. The three in common use — SOC 1, SOC 2 and SOC 3 — answer different questions for different readers.
:::

The numbers invite the reader to rank them, as though SOC 3 were a beginner's SOC 2 and SOC 1 the serious one. They are not a ladder. SOC 1 and SOC 2 cover different subject matter, and SOC 3 is a public version of a SOC 2 rather than a weaker one. Choosing the right report starts with knowing which question each was built to answer.

## What each report answers

**SOC 1** reports on a service organisation's controls that are relevant to its user entities' internal control over financial reporting (ICFR). It is performed under SSAE 18 (AT-C section 320), and its audience is the user entity's own management and their financial-statement auditors. It is a restricted-use report. A payroll bureau or a claims processor produces a SOC 1 because its processing feeds directly into its customers' financial statements. SOC 1 is not the "financial" report you hand a prospect to prove security — it is specifically about financial-reporting controls.

**SOC 2** reports on controls relevant to the [Trust Services Criteria](/frameworks/trust-services-criteria/): security, which is mandatory, and optionally availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. It is performed under AT-C section 205 and is a detailed report — it sets out the system and the controls, and in a Type II report the auditor's tests of operating effectiveness and their results, the [evidence](/compliance/evidence-that-holds-up/) that lets a reader weigh the controls rather than take an assertion on trust. It is restricted-use, shared with management, customers and regulators, typically under NDA. This is the report an enterprise security team means when it asks a vendor for "your SOC report".

**SOC 3** covers the same subject matter and the same Trust Services Criteria as a SOC 2, but is a short, general-use report. It carries the auditor's opinion and management's assertion but omits the system description, the control listing and the test results. Stripped of that detail, it can be distributed freely — published on a website as a public trust summary. A SOC 3 is a public companion to a SOC 2, not a lower grade of assurance on the same criteria.

Beyond these three, the AICPA also defines reports such as SOC for Cybersecurity and SOC for Supply Chain. They serve narrower purposes and sit outside the SOC 1 / SOC 2 / SOC 3 comparison this entry draws.

| Subject / criteria | Controls relevant to user entities' internal control over financial reporting (ICFR) | Trust Services Criteria — security, plus optional availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy | Same subject and criteria as SOC 2 |
| Standard | SSAE 18 (AT-C 320) | AT-C 205 | AT-C 205 |
| Audience & distribution | User management and their financial-statement auditors; restricted-use | Management, customers and regulators; restricted-use, usually under NDA | Anyone; general-use, freely publishable |
| Level of detail | Detailed: system and controls; a Type II adds operating-effectiveness tests and results | Detailed: system and controls; a Type II adds operating-effectiveness tests and results | Short: opinion and assertion only, no description, control listing or results |
| Type I / II | Applies | Applies | Does not apply |
| Typical use | Given to a customer's auditors when your service touches their financial statements | Shared with customers and prospects reviewing your security | Public trust summary on a website |

## Type I and Type II

A further distinction cuts across SOC 1 and SOC 2 — but not SOC 3. A Type I report tests whether the controls were suitably designed at a single point in time; a Type II tests whether they also operated effectively across a period, commonly three to twelve months. Enterprise procurement asking for "a SOC 2" almost always means a Type II, because a Type I speaks only to the suitability of design at a point in time, not to operating effectiveness.

:::def term="Type I and Type II" ref="4.4"
Two forms a SOC 1 or SOC 2 report can take. A Type I opinion addresses the suitability of the design of controls at a point in time; a Type II addresses design and operating effectiveness over a period, and so carries the auditor's tests and their results. SOC 3 has neither form. How the two bear on [the management assertion](/compliance/management-assertion/) is set out separately.
:::

When you rely on a counterparty's SOC report, check the Type first and the period it covers second. A Type I, or a Type II over a short or stale window, supports far less weight than a clean cover page implies.

## Where it goes wrong

**"SOC 2 certified."** There is no such thing. SOC 2 is an attestation — an opinion expressed in a report by a licensed CPA firm — not a certification, and nothing is issued that an organisation is "certified" against. The certifiable security standard is [ISO 27001](/frameworks/iso-27001-vs-soc-2/); where a claim of certification is what matters, that is the one to reach for. Saying "SOC 2 certified" signals to a knowledgeable reader that the speaker has not read one.

**Reaching for SOC 1 when you mean security.** SOC 1 is about controls over financial reporting, not information security. A prospect asking about your security posture wants a SOC 2. Commissioning a SOC 1 to answer a security questionnaire spends real money on the wrong report and answers a question nobody asked.

**Treating SOC 3 as a lesser SOC 2.** A SOC 3 is not a cut-price or entry-level SOC 2. It is examined against the same criteria and carries the same opinion; what it drops is the confidential detail, so that it can be published. Reading the absence of a system description as weaker assurance mistakes distribution for rigour.

**Ignoring Type I versus Type II when relying on a report.** A Type I says only that the controls were designed suitably on one day. Accepting it as evidence that controls operated across the whole year is a common and consequential misread. Check the Type, then the period it covers, before you lean on someone else's report.

## Common questions

:::faq
### A customer is asking for "our SOC report" — which one do they mean?

Almost always a SOC 2, and almost always a Type II. SOC 1 comes up only when your service affects the customer's financial statements — a payroll or billing processor, say — in which case their auditors, not their security team, will ask. If in doubt, ask whether they want it for a financial-statement audit (SOC 1) or a vendor security review (SOC 2).

### Can I say we are "SOC 2 certified"?

No. SOC 2 produces an attestation report with an auditor's opinion, not a certificate, so "certified" is incorrect and reads as a tell that the report has not been understood. Say instead that you "have a SOC 2 report" or "completed a SOC 2 Type II examination". The certifiable standard is ISO 27001.

### Is a SOC 3 weaker than a SOC 2?

No. A SOC 3 is assessed against the same Trust Services Criteria and carries the same auditor's opinion as the SOC 2 it accompanies. It simply leaves out the system description, control listing and test results so that it can be shared publicly. Treat it as the public summary, not a lower tier of assurance.
:::

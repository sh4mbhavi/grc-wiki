:::lede
A risk workshop is a **facilitated session that surfaces and sizes risks with
the people who own the work**. Its real output is the argument in the room, not
just the list that leaves it. Brainstorming is one of the techniques IEC 31010
catalogues, and a workshop applies it in a structured form.
:::

A workshop earns its place when the risk cannot be read off a document — when it
lives in the heads of the people doing the work and has to be talked out. The
value is proportional to how honestly the room disagrees.

## Who is in the room

The room needs two things at once: first-line owners, who know where the work
actually breaks, and second-line facilitation, who keep the conversation honest
and stop it drifting. Balance is the whole problem. Too senior and nobody will
contradict the person who signs the appraisals; too junior and nothing decided
in the room can be committed to afterwards.

The workable mix is people close enough to the process to see the failure and
senior enough to own the treatment, run by a facilitator who owns none of it.
Keep it small — six to ten — so every voice has room. A group larger than that
turns into an audience.

## The facilitation problems that ruin it

Most of what wrecks a workshop is social, not analytical. Four failures recur.

:::terms
Anchoring :: The first number spoken sets the range everyone then argues around. Ask for the consequence before anyone offers a score, and take the score last.
Groupthink :: The highest-paid person's opinion quietly becomes the room's opinion. The finding stops being the group's assessment and turns into theirs, unchallenged.
The loudest voice :: One confident owner crowds out three quieter ones who sit closer to the work. Airtime is not evidence; redistributing it is the facilitator's job.
Scope drift :: The room slides into solutioning — designing the fix — before the risks are even listed. Park every proposed control on a separate sheet and go back to naming risks.
:::

:::def term="Anchoring" ref="3.1"
The tendency for the first figure said aloud to fix the range every later
estimate clusters around. In a workshop it is countered by scoring last, after
the cause and consequence are agreed.
:::

## Turning a workshop into register entries

A workshop whose output never reaches the register was a meeting. Every risk
should leave the room as three things: a statement with a cause, an event and a
consequence; a single named owner; and a provisional score that can be revised
later. The facilitator's real task is conversion — turning an hour of argument
into rows that land in the register at
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/). Talk that is not written down as a row is
talk that did not happen.

## Questions people ask

:::faq
### Who should attend a risk workshop?
The people who own the work and one facilitator who does not. First-line process owners supply where it breaks; a second-line facilitator keeps the session honest and converts the talk into register rows. Keep it to six to ten so everyone speaks. A room too senior will not contradict itself, and a room too junior cannot commit to a treatment.

### How long should a risk workshop be?
Ninety minutes is usually the ceiling for useful attention. Past that the scoring gets careless and the quieter owners stop contributing. Cover fewer risks properly rather than the whole register badly — a second session beats one long one.

### How do you stop groupthink in a risk workshop?
Take the scores last, after the cause and consequence are agreed, so the first figure said aloud cannot anchor the rest, and have the most senior person speak after the others rather than first. Then invite the disagreement explicitly. A workshop in which nobody contradicts anybody has told you nothing you did not already know.
:::

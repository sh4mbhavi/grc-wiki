:::lede
Security awareness and education is the programme that **turns written policy
into day-to-day behaviour** — teaching staff to recognise the situations that
lead to incidents and, more importantly, to report them quickly.
:::

It is a required control — ISO 27001 Annex A 6.3, the NIST AT family, and a line
in nearly every regulation — but it is the one most often run as a compliance
box. An annual slide deck with a quiz at the end satisfies the auditor's
question "do you train staff" and changes almost nothing about what people do
when a convincing email arrives.

The distinction that matters is between *awareness* and *culture*. Awareness is
knowing the rule. Culture is a workplace where reporting a mistake is met with
thanks rather than blame, so people raise the click they just made instead of
hiding it. A programme that drives the first without the second produces staff
who know they did something wrong and say nothing.

## What a working programme contains

:::terms
Role-based content :: Developers get secure-coding and dependency risk; finance gets invoice fraud and payment authorisation; executives get targeted social engineering and travel risk. One generic course for everyone wastes the audience it does not fit.
Onboarding and ongoing :: Training at joining, before access to sensitive systems, then short and frequent reinforcement rather than one long annual event people click through.
Phishing simulation :: Realistic simulated phishing sent on a schedule, used to teach and to measure the report rate — not to punish, and not to generate a "gotcha" statistic for a board slide.
Just-in-time prompts :: A warning at the moment of risk — an external-sender banner, a large-payment confirmation step — which changes behaviour more reliably than a course remembered months later.
Metrics that mean something :: Time to report a simulated phish, proportion of recipients who report, repeat-clicker trend. Completion percentage measures attendance, not risk.
:::

:::def term="Human risk management" ref="3.9"
The reframing of awareness as a risk domain: identify which behaviours cause
loss, measure them, target interventions at the highest-risk groups, and track
the residual. It moves the programme from "everyone did the training" to
"the behaviour that causes incidents is trending down".
:::

## Measuring it honestly

The click rate on a phishing simulation is the number every board asks for and
the weakest one to lead with. It moves with email difficulty, time of day and
seasonal workload, and a low rate can simply mean the lure was obvious.

The stronger measure is the **report rate** and the **time to first report**. An
organisation where 60% of recipients report a phish within ten minutes can
absorb the 5% who clicked, because the incident response team gets its warning
early. One where nobody reports is exposed regardless of the click rate, because
the first sign of trouble will be the breach itself.

## Where security awareness fails

**Annual-course thinking.** One 45-minute module, once a year, completion tracked
to 100%, no measurement of whether anything changed. It is the format auditors
have learned to look past.

**Phishing as punishment.** Clickers are named to their managers or assigned
extra training as a penalty. Reporting drops immediately, because owning up now
has a cost.

**No role targeting.** The finance team, who are the actual target of invoice
fraud, get the same generic content as everyone else and nothing about the
scenario that will be used against them.

**Blame culture.** The policy says "report immediately", and the last person who
did was disciplined. The next person will not.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is security awareness training?

A programme that teaches staff to recognise and respond to security risks in
their actual work — phishing, social engineering, data handling, physical
security — and to report suspected incidents quickly. It is a required control
under ISO 27001, NIST and most regulations, and is intended to change behaviour,
not just record attendance.

### What is the difference between security awareness and security culture?

Awareness is whether people know the rules. Culture is whether the environment
makes following them, and admitting mistakes, the natural choice. A programme can
raise awareness while a blame culture suppresses the reporting that makes
awareness useful.

### What is the best metric for a security awareness programme?

The rate and speed at which staff report suspicious messages, not the click rate
on phishing simulations. A high report rate gives the incident team early
warning; the click rate is noisy and easily gamed by making the simulation
obvious. Completion percentage measures attendance only.

### Should phishing simulation results be used to discipline staff?

No. Using them punitively causes people to stop reporting, which removes the
programme's main benefit. Simulations should be framed as practice, with
repeat-clicker patterns handled as a coaching and targeting problem rather than
a disciplinary one.
:::

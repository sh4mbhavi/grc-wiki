:::lede
Incident response is the **prepared process for detecting a security incident,
containing it, recovering normal operation and learning from it**, written,
rehearsed and resourced before anything happens, because an incident is the
worst time to design the response.
:::

The distinction from ordinary operations is that an incident has a confirmed or
strongly suspected breach of confidentiality, integrity or availability, and a
clock. Regulators attach deadlines (72 hours to notify a data-protection
authority under GDPR, tighter still under DORA and the SEC rules), and those
clocks start at detection, not at the point the organisation feels ready to
talk. A response that is being invented while the clock runs will miss them.

Incident response also has an evidence obligation the rest of security does not.
Actions taken during containment can destroy the forensic record of how the
attacker got in, and can prejudice a later legal or insurance claim. The plan
has to say when to preserve and when to prioritise recovery, and who decides.

## The lifecycle

NIST SP 800-61 gives the model almost every plan follows.

:::terms
Preparation :: The plan, the playbooks, the contact tree, the retainer with a forensics firm, the logging that will actually be needed, and the exercises that prove all of it works. Everything expensive happens here, before the incident.
Detection and analysis :: Recognising that an event is an incident, from monitoring, a user report or a third party, and establishing scope: which systems, which data, how far in.
Containment :: Stopping the spread. Short-term isolation first (disconnect, disable, block), then a longer-term hold while eradication is planned. The point to decide whether to preserve evidence or move straight to recovery.
Eradication :: Removing the cause (the foothold, the malicious account, the vulnerability that was used) so the same route cannot be taken again immediately.
Recovery :: Restoring systems to normal, from known-good backups where needed, with heightened monitoring to catch a return.
Post-incident review :: A blameless account of what happened, what worked, what did not, and the specific changes now owned by named people with dates. The phase skipped most often and the one that pays back.
:::

:::def term="Playbook" ref="4.13"
A step-by-step procedure for one incident type: ransomware, business email
compromise, a lost laptop, a public data exposure. The plan is the framework
that applies to everything; the playbook is what the responder actually follows
at 3am for this scenario.
:::

## Severity and escalation

Every plan needs a severity scale defined in advance (typically SEV1 to SEV3 or
similar) keyed to concrete criteria: data categories involved, systems
affected, regulatory exposure, whether the incident is ongoing. The scale drives
who is woken, whether legal and communications are engaged, and whether the
board is told. Deciding severity during the incident, with no scale, produces
either panic escalation of everything or a serious breach handled by one tired
analyst.

## Where incident response fails

**A plan never exercised.** A document exists, approved and filed, and the first
time anyone reads it properly is during a live incident. The contact numbers are
stale, the roles are unclear and the forensics retainer was never signed.

**Containment that destroys evidence.** Machines are wiped and rebuilt for speed,
and with them goes the only record of how the attacker got in: the same
route is used again, and the insurance claim is weakened.

**No lessons-learned discipline.** The incident is closed, everyone is
exhausted, the review is skipped or produces vague actions with no owner. The
next incident is the same incident.

**Regulatory clock missed.** Nobody owned the notification assessment, so the
72-hour window under GDPR passed while the technical team was still working the
containment.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What are the phases of incident response?

The NIST model has six: preparation; detection and analysis; containment;
eradication; recovery; and post-incident review (lessons learned). Some
frameworks compress eradication and recovery into one, giving a four-phase
version, but the sequence and intent are the same.

### What is the difference between an incident response plan and a playbook?

The plan is the overall framework (roles, severity scale, escalation, legal and
regulatory obligations) that applies to any incident. A playbook is a specific
procedure for one scenario, such as ransomware or business email compromise,
that a responder follows step by step. An organisation needs one plan and
several playbooks.

### How quickly must a data breach be reported?

It depends on the regime. Under the EU GDPR, a personal-data breach must be
notified to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of the organisation
becoming aware of it, unless it is unlikely to result in risk to individuals.
Other regimes (DORA, the SEC cyber rules, sector regulators, contracts) impose
their own, often shorter, clocks.

### What is a post-incident review?

A structured, blameless examination held after the incident is closed, covering
the timeline, what helped and hindered the response, the root cause, and a set
of specific improvements each assigned to an owner and a date. It is what turns
an incident into a reduction in future risk rather than just a bad week.
:::

:::lede
Risk and compliance **use the same controls to answer different questions**.
Risk asks what could hurt the organisation and whether the exposure sits within
appetite. Compliance asks what the organisation is required to do and whether it
can prove it did.
:::

Most of the time the two answers agree, which is why the same overlapping team
runs both off one register. A single quarterly access review treats the risk of
excessive access and evidences the obligation to review it - one control, logged
twice. The distinction only becomes load-bearing when the two answers disagree:
when a control passes its compliance test and the risk behind it is still outside
appetite, or when a risk the business is willing to carry runs straight into an
obligation it is not allowed to carry.

## The comparison

:::versus a="Risk" a-badge="EXPOSURE & APPETITE" b="Compliance" b-badge="OBLIGATION & EVIDENCE"
Begins with what could go wrong, sizes it against the objectives and asks
whether the residual sits inside appetite. The register is never finished; the
exposure keeps moving.
+++
Begins from the obligations that bind it - law, contract, standard - maps each to
a control and keeps the proof it operated. Each obligation is either evidenced
or open.
:::

:::attrs a="Risk" b="Compliance"
Driven by :: Exposure and appetite :: Obligation and evidence
The question :: "What could hurt us, and is it within appetite?" :: "What are we required to do, and can we prove it?"
Measured on :: Residual exposure against appetite :: Obligations evidenced against obligations open
When it is "done" :: Never - re-scored as the exposure moves :: Per obligation, when its evidence is current - the system itself never stops
Fails when :: Exposure sits above appetite and nobody owns the treatment :: A control has no evidence, or an obligation no control
:::

ISO 31000 defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives and has the
organisation evaluate it against risk criteria, which typically encode the
appetite governance has set. ISO 37301 builds the other side into an ongoing
management system - one of monitoring, evaluation and improvement - whose unit of
work is the compliance obligation: a requirement you must meet or a commitment you
have chosen to. COSO supplies the join: a control is the response to a risk to an
objective, and the same control is often what a compliance regime points to as
proof. The controls are shared. The questions asked of them are not.

## The overlap

In most teams the two live in one register, and a control carries two keys: the
risk it treats and the obligation it evidences. The access review above is the
ordinary case - raised as a treatment for an access risk, cited as evidence
against an access-control obligation, closed once with a single artefact.

Running them as two registers is the common mistake in a young programme. It
produces two control inventories that drift apart, two owners for the same
review and a control that is marked effective on the compliance side while the
risk it was meant to reduce sits open on the other. One register with two keys -
however it is physically stored - is the arrangement that tends to survive an
audit.

## Where they pull apart

The value is in the two cases where a clean answer on one side is worthless on
the other.

A fully compliant control can still leave risk above appetite. Compliance is
a floor, not a ceiling. Multi-factor authentication is required by policy and the
evidence shows it running - the obligation is met and the test passes. If it is
not enforced on a legacy login path, the account-takeover exposure is still
outside appetite. The compliance answer is green; the risk answer is red; both
are correct. Meeting the obligation says only that the obligation's bar was
cleared, never that the bar was set where your exposure actually is.

An accepted risk can still breach an obligation you cannot accept your way out
of. Acceptance - retaining a risk by informed decision, in ISO 31000's terms - is
a legitimate treatment: the business may formally decide to carry a risk. That
decision has no force against a binding obligation. A retention rule fixed in law is breached whether or not the risk
committee signed off carrying it - you can accept a risk, you cannot accept a
statute. When acceptance is the treatment, the compliance question has to be
asked separately, because it is the one the acceptance cannot answer.

## How they report

The two answers go to different rooms, in different shapes.

Risk reports exposure against appetite to a risk committee: where the residual
sits, which items have moved and what is above appetite still waiting on a
treatment. The unit is a risk, and the question in the room is whether the
organisation is carrying more than it agreed to.

Compliance reports obligations met against obligations open to a compliance or
audit committee: which obligations have current evidence, which have gaps and
which are in breach. The unit is an obligation, and the question in the room is
whether anything the organisation is bound by is unproven or unmet.

An item can be comfortable in one report and alarming in the other. That is not
a reporting error - it is the two functions doing their separate jobs on the same
control.

## The short version

Ask which question is on the table. If it is "are we carrying more than we agreed
to", it is risk, and the answer is measured against appetite. If it is "can we
prove we meet what binds us", it is compliance, and the answer is measured
against the obligation. The controls are the same; treat the two answers as
independent, because the day they disagree is the day both matter.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is compliance part of risk management?

It depends who you ask, and both answers are defensible. To a risk function
working in COSO or ISO 31000 terms, non-compliance is one category of risk, so
compliance sits inside enterprise risk as a subset. To a compliance function
under ISO 37301, compliance is its own management system with obligations that
exist whether or not anyone has scored them as risky - a peer, not a subset.

In practice it is both. The obligation exists on its own footing; the exposure
created by failing it is managed as a risk. Teams that insist on one framing
usually do it to settle who owns the register, which is a reporting-line question
dressed up as a definitional one.

### Can you be compliant but not secure?

Routinely. Compliance is a floor: it confirms the obligation's bar was cleared,
not that the bar matches your exposure. A control can pass every compliance test
and still leave a risk above appetite, because the obligation demanded less than
your situation needs - an old standard, a narrow contract clause, a control that
runs but not everywhere it has to.

The reverse happens too. A well-secured system can hold an obligation open
because the evidence was never captured in the form an assessor accepts. Secure
and compliant are correlated, not the same measurement.

### Which comes first, risk or compliance?

It turns on the trigger. If a regulator, contract or standard already binds you,
compliance comes first - the obligation exists regardless of how your risk
assessment comes out, and you cannot risk-assess your way out of it. If you are
deciding where to spend a fixed control budget, risk comes first, because it
tells you which threats to objectives are worth the money.

In a mature programme the ordering dissolves: obligations and risks feed one
register, and each control is justified by both the exposure it reduces and the
obligation it satisfies.
:::

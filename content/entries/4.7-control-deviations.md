:::lede
A control deviation is an **instance in the tested sample where the control did
not operate as designed**. In a SOC 2 report these are called exceptions, and a
single one can put the whole control's conclusion in question.
:::

A deviation is a finding about the control, not about the one instance that
failed. The auditor never sees the whole population (they test a sample and
conclude back to everything that sample stands for), so a deviation bears on the
conclusion for the whole population, not just the one instance that failed. How
you treat it, more than the deviation itself, is what the auditor watches next.

## A deviation is not a waiver

The two get confused because both describe a control that was not met, and the
confusion is expensive. A policy waiver is a decision made before the fact: a
named approver signs off, in advance, that a specific gap will run for a bounded
period with a compensating control. A deviation is a failure discovered after
the fact, in testing, that nobody authorised.

Recasting a deviation as a waiver once the auditor has found it is the move that
turns a control finding into an integrity finding. Back-dating an approval to
cover an instance that already failed is not exception management; it is
fabricated evidence, and it follows a programme far longer than the deviation
ever would.

:::def term="Policy waiver" ref="3.10"
A planned, pre-authorised and time-bound exception, signed before the gap opens
and paired with a compensating control. A deviation is the opposite in every
respect: unplanned, unauthorised and found only after the control has already
failed.
:::

## What one deviation says about the population

The auditor tests a sample (say 25 change tickets out of a year's several
thousand) and concludes on the whole population from it. One deviation in that
sample of 25 is not one late change to be corrected in place; it puts the
operating-effectiveness conclusion for every change the sample stood for in
question, and commonly prompts the auditor to expand testing to see how far it
reaches. The figures here are illustrative, and this sampling is judgemental
rather than statistical, so it supports a conclusion about the population, not a
precise failure rate: estimating an actual rate would need a valid probability
sample.

This is why "we fixed the ticket" is the wrong instinct. Correcting the sampled
instance leaves the population (and the conclusion the sample was testing)
exactly where they were.

## Isolated or systematic

The question that decides the response is whether the deviation is a one-off or
the visible edge of a control that does not work.

:::terms
Isolated :: A single instance failed for a reason that does not recur: a one-time manual slip, a cycle with the reviewer on leave and no cover. The design holds; one operation of it did not.
Systematic :: The deviation reflects a design or operating failure that will recur: the control has no step that would have caught the case, or the step exists and is routinely skipped. Every untested instance is now suspect.
:::

Root cause is what tells the two apart, and you cannot assert "isolated" without
having found one. A deviation labelled isolated with no analysis behind it reads
to an auditor as systematic with the analysis skipped.

## The management response

A response answers why the control failed and what changes because of it; it does
not argue that the failure should not have counted. Three parts carry it.

**Root cause.** Why the instance failed, established by analysis rather than
assumed. Until it is known, isolated versus systematic cannot be answered and the
remediation is a guess.

**Remediation.** What changes so the failure does not recur, across the
population and not only for the sampled instance. A fix that touches one ticket
and leaves the process untouched has remediated nothing.

**A statement of what failed.** Whether the control's design was inadequate or a
sound design was not operated. The two lead to different fixes, and an honest
answer to which it was is worth more than any argument that the deviation should
not count.

## Where the response goes wrong

Four moves recur, and each one makes the finding worse.

**Arguing it away.** Spending the response explaining why the deviation does not
really count instead of finding why it happened. The auditor has already recorded
it; the only open question is the cause, and the effort spent on the argument is
the effort that would have shrunk the finding.

**Back-dating a waiver.** Producing an approval, dated before the failure, to
reclassify a deviation as a planned exception. This converts a routine control
finding into an integrity finding and is the most damaging response available.

**Assuming one means one.** Treating a single exception in a small sample as a
single isolated problem. One deviation in a sample of 25 speaks to the whole
population the sample stood for, and because the sample is small and judgemental
it rarely proves the problem is contained to the instance that failed.

**Fixing the instance, not the population.** Remediating the sampled item and
leaving every other instance as it was. The sample stood for the population; the
response has to reach the same population.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is a control deviation?
A control deviation is an instance, found while testing a control, where the control did not operate as it was designed to. In SOC 2 and other examination reports these are called exceptions. Because the auditor tests a sample and concludes on the whole population, a deviation bears on whether the control operated effectively across everything the sample stood for, not just the instance that failed, so it is not a single fault to be corrected in place.

### What is the difference between a deviation and a policy exception?
Timing and authorisation. A policy exception, or [waiver](/risk/policy-waivers/), is planned: a named approver signs off in advance that a specific gap will run for a bounded period, with a compensating control. A deviation is unplanned: a failure discovered after the fact, in testing, that nobody authorised. Reclassifying a deviation as an exception once an auditor has found it (by back-dating the approval) turns a control finding into an integrity finding.

### Does one exception fail a SOC 2 control?
Not automatically. A single exception does not by itself mean the criterion was not met; the service auditor evaluates the cause of the exception, whether it is isolated or systematic and what it implies for the rest of the population before concluding. One exception can contribute to a qualified opinion when its effect is material, and it can also be judged isolated and immaterial, which is why the root cause and the response matter more than the count.
:::

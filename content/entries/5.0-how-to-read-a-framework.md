:::lede
Four different kinds of document are called frameworks, and they are not
alternatives. Which kind you are holding decides what its output is worth to
whoever asked for it.
:::

The question "which framework should we do" is nearly always answered by who is
asking and what they will accept, not by the merits of the documents.

## The four kinds

:::terms
Certifiable standard :: Specifies requirements a management system must meet. An accredited body audits against it and issues a certificate. ISO/IEC 27001, ISO 9001, ISO 22301.
Attestation criteria :: Criteria a practitioner tests your described controls against, producing a report rather than a certificate. SOC 2 against the Trust Services Criteria.
Descriptive framework :: A vocabulary and structure for describing where you are. Not a bar to clear, and nothing certifies against it. NIST CSF 2.0, with its six functions.
Control model :: A model of how control and risk should reach a governing body. Used to design a programme, not to test one. COSO ERM, the IIA's Three Lines Model.
:::

## The two questions that sort them

**What does it produce?** A certificate travels and can be published. A report
is read, usually under NDA, and says considerably more. A profile or a maturity
score says where you are and binds you to nothing. A model produces a design.

**Who audits it?** An accredited certification body, a licensed practitioner,
yourself, or nobody. This is what determines whether the output means anything
to a third party — a self-assessed anything is worth exactly the trust already
placed in you.

## Reading the document itself

Three things to find before anything else.

**The scope clause.** Every certifiable standard has one, and it is where the
real negotiation happens. A certificate covering one product line is not a
certificate covering the company, and the scope statement is printed on it.

**The shall/should boundary.** In a standard, "shall" is a requirement and
"should" is a recommendation. Auditors test the first and ask about the second.
Reading a standard without noticing which is which produces a programme that
gold-plates recommendations and misses requirements.

**The annex's status.** ISO 27001's Annex A is a reference control set you
justify including or excluding, not a checklist you complete. Treating it as
mandatory is the single most expensive misreading in the discipline.

:::def term="Accreditation" ref="5.1"
The oversight of the certification bodies themselves, by a national
accreditation body. An unaccredited certificate is a document a company bought.
:::

## What overlaps and what does not

The controls overlap heavily — access, change, vulnerabilities, incidents,
vendors, backups. The shells do not: ISO wants a management system with internal
audit and management review; SOC 2 wants a system description and the auditor's
own testing; NIST CSF wants neither and gives you a profile.

Budget for one control programme and as many reporting shells as you have
buyers. See [5.2](/frameworks/building-a-crosswalk/) for the artefact that makes
that affordable.

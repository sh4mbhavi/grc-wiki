:::lede
In a company of thirty, one person does all of it alongside another job. In a
company of three thousand, the same work is four teams with different reporting
lines. The seats change; the artefacts do not.
:::

The useful way to read an organisation chart here is to ask which of the four
artefacts each seat is accountable for, and who receives their output. Titles
vary far more than that does.

## The seats, roughly in the order they appear

:::terms
GRC analyst :: The first dedicated hire. Runs the register, chases evidence, answers questionnaires, keeps the policy set current. The job is 70% writing things down so somebody else can act on them.
Compliance manager :: Owns the obligation register and the audit relationship. Appears when there is a second framework, or a first regulator.
Risk manager :: Owns the register, the appetite statement and the reporting into committee. Appears when the register outgrows the analyst who started it.
Privacy lead / DPO :: Owns a distinct obligation set with its own regulator and its own artefacts. In the EU the DPO role has statutory independence requirements that make it a poor fit for a general compliance seat.
Third-party risk analyst :: Owns supplier due diligence and the ongoing monitoring. Usually the first specialisation, because the volume is relentless.
Head of GRC :: Owns the programme, the budget and the board narrative. Answers for the function's opinion, not its paperwork.
:::

## Who they report to

Most commonly the CISO, in technology companies where the obligations are
security-led. Increasingly the General Counsel where the obligation set is
regulatory rather than technical, and occasionally the CFO where it grew out of
financial controls.

The reporting line matters less than one thing: whoever it is must not also own
the systems being assessed. A GRC function reporting to the engineering leader
whose controls it tests is second line in name only.

## The trap in a small company

One person writing the policy, running the access review, and signing the
attestation that the access review happened is performing a self-review. Under
about a hundred staff this is unavoidable and everybody knows it.

The way to handle it is not to pretend otherwise. Record it in the register as
an accepted risk with a named approver, and get a second pair of eyes - a peer,
a board member, an external reviewer - on the attestations that leave the
building. An auditor who finds the acceptance already written respects it. One
who finds it themselves writes a finding.

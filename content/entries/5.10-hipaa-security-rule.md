:::lede
The **HIPAA Security Rule** is the part of US federal law that sets national standards for protecting electronic protected health information — ePHI — through administrative, physical and technical safeguards. It is technology-neutral and scales to the size and complexity of the organisation.
:::

HIPAA — the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act — was enacted in 1996; the Security Rule itself was finalised in 2003, with compliance required from 2005. It is codified at 45 CFR Part 160 and Part 164, and it governs one thing narrowly: ePHI, health information created, held or moved in electronic form. The broader Privacy Rule covers protected health information in every form, paper included, and the two are routinely conflated. This entry is the Security Rule.

The distinction that matters most sits underneath all of it: this is law, not a contract. Non-compliance is enforced by a federal regulator, and the safeguards are written to be assessed as legal obligations rather than as a supplier's promise. Everything below follows from that.

## What it protects and who it binds

The Rule protects ePHI, and it binds two classes of party. **Covered entities** are health plans, health-care clearinghouses and health-care providers who transmit health information electronically in connection with a transaction for which HHS has adopted a standard. **Business associates** are the vendors that create, receive, maintain or transmit ePHI on a covered entity's behalf — a cloud host, a billing processor, a managed-service provider.

Business associates were not always directly on the hook. The 2013 HIPAA Omnibus Rule, implementing the HITECH Act, made them **directly liable** to the regulator for the Security Rule, rather than liable only through the contract with the covered entity that engaged them. A vendor handling ePHI is answerable to the regulator in its own right, and a covered entity that treats its vendors' compliance as the vendors' problem alone has misread the position.

## The safeguards

The Rule groups its standards into three families of safeguards, plus organisational requirements and a documentation regime.

:::terms
Administrative safeguards :: The largest group — assigned security responsibility, workforce training, contingency planning and the required risk analysis that governs how ePHI is protected.
Physical safeguards :: Controls over physical access to the systems and facilities that hold ePHI — facility access controls; workstation use; workstation security; and device and media controls.
Technical safeguards :: The technology controls on the ePHI itself — access control, audit controls, integrity, authentication and transmission security.
Organisational requirements :: The contracts that bind the parties who handle ePHI, most visibly the business-associate agreement.
Policies, procedures and documentation :: The written policies behind the safeguards and the records that evidence them, retained for six years.
:::

The documentation family is not administrative overhead. It is the raw material a compliance function turns into evidence — see [what compliance produces](/compliance/what-compliance-produces/) (4.0) — and an unwritten control is, for enforcement purposes, close to an absent one.

## Required and addressable specifications

Each standard carries one or more **implementation specifications**, and every specification is marked either **required** or **addressable**. The single most expensive misreading of the Security Rule is treating "addressable" as "optional". It is not.

For an addressable specification an organisation must assess whether the safeguard is a reasonable and appropriate control in its own environment, and then do one of two things: implement it; or, where it is genuinely not reasonable and appropriate, document why not and implement an equivalent alternative measure where reasonable to do so. Assessing nothing and implementing nothing is not one of the options. Reading specification language precisely is a general framework skill, not a HIPAA quirk — see [how to read a framework](/frameworks/how-to-read-a-framework/) (5.0).

:::def term="Addressable" ref="5.0"
A status on an implementation specification meaning the organisation must decide whether the safeguard is reasonable and appropriate for its environment, then either implement it or record why it is not reasonable and appropriate and put an equivalent alternative measure in place where reasonable. It does not mean optional and it does not license inaction.
:::

## The risk analysis is the foundation

A **risk analysis** — an accurate and thorough assessment of the risks to the confidentiality, integrity and availability of ePHI — is itself a required administrative safeguard, at 45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A). It is not a preliminary; it is the control the rest of the programme is built on. Which addressable specifications are reasonable, where scarce effort should go first, what "reasonable and appropriate" means for this organisation — all of it is decided from the risk analysis.

This is [what the risk function produces](/risk/what-the-risk-function-produces/) (3.0) pointed at a single dataset. It is also, done badly or not at all, one of the most common findings in enforcement: a risk analysis that covers one system and ignores the rest, or that was run once and never revisited, is a recurring theme in settled cases.

## Enforcement, and the certification that does not exist

The Security Rule is enforced by the **HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR)**. Because HIPAA is a law, the consequences of non-compliance are legal: civil monetary penalties tiered by culpability — from a genuinely unknowing breach up to wilful neglect — and, in some cases, criminal penalties. That is the sharp contrast with [PCI DSS](/frameworks/pci-dss/) (5.11), which imposes similar-looking security requirements but binds through a private contract with the card brands rather than through statute — different enforcer and different teeth.

One consequence is worth stating plainly, because it costs organisations real money: there is no official **HIPAA certification**. No government body certifies HIPAA compliance, and no certificate confers it. An organisation can undergo a third-party assessment or make an attestation, and either can be useful, but "HIPAA certified" is not a government credential and a vendor advertising one is describing an assessment at best.

## Where it goes wrong

**Reading "addressable" as "optional".** Addressable specifications demand an assessment and then either implementation or a documented, equivalent alternative. Skipping one because it is "only addressable" is a straightforward finding waiting to be made.

**Skimping the risk analysis.** A required safeguard treated as a formality — narrow scope, stale date, no follow-through — undermines every decision that should have flowed from it, and it is among the most cited failings in enforcement.

**Assuming a "HIPAA certification" exists.** It does not. Buying or claiming one wastes money and can mislead partners; the honest statement is that an independent assessment was performed against the Rule.

**Confusing the Security Rule with the Privacy Rule.** The Security Rule governs ePHI — electronic data. The Privacy Rule governs protected health information in every form, and covers uses and disclosures the Security Rule never touches. A programme built for one is not automatically compliant with the other.

**Forgetting business associates are directly liable.** Since the 2013 Omnibus Rule a vendor handling ePHI answers to the regulator itself, not only to the covered entity that hired it. A business-associate agreement allocates responsibility; it does not move a vendor's ePHI outside the Rule's reach.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is HIPAA a law or a framework?
HIPAA is a US federal law, enacted in 1996, and the Security Rule is a regulation issued under it. That is why it is enforced by a regulator with civil and, in some cases, criminal penalties, rather than through a contract the way PCI DSS is. It is not a voluntary framework you adopt; if you handle ePHI as a covered entity or business associate, it applies to you.

### Does "addressable" mean I can skip the control?
No. Addressable means you must assess whether the safeguard is reasonable and appropriate for your environment, then either implement it or document why it is not and put an equivalent measure in place where reasonable. The one response that is never compliant is doing nothing and recording nothing.

### Can my company get HIPAA certified?
No — there is no official HIPAA certification and no government body issues one. You can have an independent assessor evaluate your safeguards against the Security Rule, and that can be worth doing, but the result is an assessment or attestation, not a government-recognised certificate. Treat any "HIPAA certified" badge as marketing.
:::

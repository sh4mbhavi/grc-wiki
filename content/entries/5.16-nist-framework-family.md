:::lede
NIST publishes a **family of security and risk documents that do different
jobs** — CSF 2.0 describes outcomes, SP 800-53 is the control catalogue,
SP 800-171 is the baseline for government contractors, the Risk Management
Framework is the process that ties them together, and the AI RMF extends the
same thinking to AI systems.
:::

The confusion is understandable, because organisations cite "NIST" as if it were
one thing. It is a publisher. Most of what it produces is voluntary guidance;
some of it becomes mandatory through a contract clause or a federal regulation,
not through NIST itself. Knowing which document someone means — and whether it
applies to you by choice or by obligation — is the first step in any conversation
that starts "we need to be NIST compliant".

## The core documents

:::terms
CSF 2.0 :: An outcome framework organised into six functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. It describes what good looks like, not how to do it, and is used to assess maturity and communicate posture. The 2024 version added Govern and made it explicitly for organisations of any size and sector.
SP 800-53 :: A catalogue of over 1,000 security and privacy controls in 20 families, with three baselines (low, moderate, high). Mandatory for US federal information systems; widely borrowed elsewhere as a control reference. This is the detail CSF points to.
SP 800-171 :: A trimmed set of requirements — 110 in revision 2, restructured in revision 3 — for non-federal organisations that handle Controlled Unclassified Information under a US government contract. Enforced through the DFARS clause and, increasingly, CMMC assessment.
SP 800-37 (RMF) :: A seven-step process — Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize, Monitor — for bringing a system into service with a documented risk decision behind it. This is the "how" that operationalises 800-53.
AI RMF :: A voluntary framework for managing risks specific to AI systems, structured as Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. It mirrors the CSF approach and is the reference most US AI-governance programmes start from.
:::

:::def term="Controlled Unclassified Information" ref="5.7"
Information the US government requires to be safeguarded but which is not
classified — contract data, technical drawings, some personal data. Handling it
under a federal contract triggers SP 800-171.
:::

## CSF and 800-53 are not alternatives

The most common mistake is treating CSF 2.0 and SP 800-53 as two options to
choose between. They are layers. CSF gives you the outcome categories and a
maturity conversation the board can follow. When you need to say *how* a category
is met, you drop to a control catalogue — 800-53, or ISO 27001 Annex A, or the
CIS Controls — and CSF 2.0 ships mappings to exactly those. Use CSF to structure
the programme and report on it; use a control set to implement it.

## Which one applies to you

- **US federal agency or system**: 800-53 and the RMF are mandatory.
- **US government contractor touching CUI**: 800-171, via your contract, with
  CMMC assessment arriving as the enforcement mechanism.
- **Everyone else**: CSF 2.0 is a free, well-recognised way to structure and
  benchmark a security programme, with no certification and no obligation. The
  AI RMF plays the same role for AI risk.

## Where NIST adoption goes wrong

**"NIST compliant" with no document named.** The requirement is written into a
policy or a contract without specifying CSF, 800-53 or 800-171, and two teams
spend a quarter building to different targets.

**CSF treated as a checklist.** The framework is a set of outcomes to assess
against, not controls to tick. Scoring every subcategory "implemented" with no
control evidence behind it produces a maturity chart that means nothing.

**800-53 adopted whole.** An organisation with no federal obligation implements
the moderate baseline in full, hundreds of controls, most irrelevant to its
risk, instead of using it as a reference to draw from.

**171 revision confusion.** A programme built to revision 2's 110 requirements is
not automatically aligned to revision 3's restructured set; the transition needs
a gap analysis.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is the difference between the NIST CSF and NIST 800-53?

The CSF is an outcome framework: six functions and a set of categories that
describe what a good security programme achieves, used for assessment and
communication. SP 800-53 is a detailed catalogue of over a thousand controls
used to implement those outcomes. CSF is the structure; 800-53 is one of the
control sets it maps to. Federal systems must use 800-53; the CSF is voluntary.

### Is NIST CSF mandatory?

Not in itself. The CSF is voluntary guidance for any organisation. It can become
contractually or legally required if a customer, regulator or sector rule points
to it, but NIST does not certify or enforce it. SP 800-53 and SP 800-171 are the
NIST documents that carry mandatory force, and only in specific federal or
contractor contexts.

### What is NIST 800-171 and who needs it?

SP 800-171 is a set of security requirements for non-federal organisations that
store or process Controlled Unclassified Information under a US government
contract. It is enforced through the DFARS contract clause and, progressively,
through CMMC assessments. If you do not handle CUI for the US government, it does
not apply to you.

### What is the NIST Risk Management Framework?

A seven-step process (Prepare, Categorize, Select, Implement, Assess, Authorize,
Monitor) defined in SP 800-37 for putting a system into operation with a formal,
documented risk-acceptance decision. It is mandatory for US federal systems and
is the process layer that turns the 800-53 control catalogue into an authorised
system.

### What is the NIST AI RMF?

A voluntary framework (NIST AI 100-1) for identifying and managing risks unique
to AI systems, organised as Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. It parallels the
CSF's structure and is the common starting reference for US AI-governance
programmes, alongside the certifiable ISO/IEC 42001.
:::

:::lede
ISO/IEC 27001 certifies that a management system exists and works, and produces
a certificate. SOC 2 attests that specific controls operated over a period, and
produces a report someone reads. They answer different questions for different
buyers.
:::

The question "which should we do first" is nearly always settled by who is
asking for it, not by the merits of either standard.

## The comparison

:::versus a="ISO/IEC 27001" a-badge="CERTIFICATION" b="SOC 2" b-badge="ATTESTATION"
An accredited body audits your ISMS against the standard and issues a
certificate you can publish. Annex A supplies a reference control set you
justify including or excluding.
+++
A licensed CPA firm tests the controls you describe against the Trust Services
Criteria and writes a report, usually shared under NDA. No fixed control list —
you describe your own, then prove them.
:::

:::attrs a="ISO/IEC 27001" b="SOC 2"
Output :: Certificate, publishable :: Auditor report, usually under NDA
Audited by :: Accredited certification body :: Licensed CPA firm
Period :: Point in time, three-year cycle :: Type I point in time; Type II a period
Fixed control list :: +Annex A, as a reference :: -None, you describe your own
Management system required :: +Yes :: -No
Recognised by :: European and global procurement :: North American technology buyers
:::

The fixed-control-list row is the structural difference and the one that surprises people.
ISO 27001 hands you a reference set of controls in Annex A and asks you to
justify inclusions and exclusions in a Statement of Applicability. SOC 2 hands
you criteria and asks you to describe the controls you have chosen to meet them.
One is a checklist you argue with; the other is an essay you must then prove.

:::def term="Statement of Applicability" ref="5.3"
The document recording which Annex A controls apply, which do not, and the
justification for each decision. Required by ISO/IEC 27001 clause 6.1.3 d).
:::

## Type I and Type II

A distinction only SOC 2 has, and the one most often got wrong in sales
conversations.

**Type I** says the controls were suitably designed at a point in time. It is a
snapshot, achievable quickly, and a sophisticated buyer will note that it proves
nothing about operation.

**Type II** says the controls operated effectively across a period — commonly
three to twelve months. This is what enterprise procurement means when it says
"SOC 2". A first Type II usually covers a shorter window, then extends to twelve
months on the next cycle.

## The overlap, and why it pays

The control sets overlap substantially. Access review, change management,
vulnerability management, incident response, vendor management and backup do
work for both, with the same evidence.

What does not transfer is the shell around them. ISO 27001 requires management
system machinery — scope, policy, risk assessment methodology, internal audit,
management review, corrective action — that SOC 2 has no equivalent of. SOC 2
requires a system description and a control matrix in a specific form, plus an
auditor's own testing, which ISO does not.

Budget for one control programme and two reporting shells. Organisations that
budget for two programmes spend roughly twice what they need to and produce two
inconsistent control inventories, which is worse than the cost.

## Choosing

If your buyers are North American technology companies, they will ask for SOC 2
and many will not recognise an ISO certificate. If they are European, or
enterprise procurement anywhere, they will ask for ISO 27001. If they are both,
do the control work once and sequence the reporting — SOC 2 Type I first for
speed, ISO 27001 next for reach, SOC 2 Type II on the following cycle.

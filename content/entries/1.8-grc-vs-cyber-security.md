:::lede
GRC and cyber security are **different disciplines that meet at the control**.
Cyber security builds and operates the defences; GRC governs, measures and
evidences them. GRC is not a technical security role.
:::

The two get merged because in a technology company they are often the same
budget and sometimes the same team. A security engineer configures the firewall;
the GRC analyst three desks away writes the standard that says the firewall must
exist, checks that it does and hands the proof to an auditor. Same subject,
different sentence: one operates, the other governs and records.

The distinction holds even where the org chart hides it, because the two roles
fail in different ways. Security fails when the defences do not stop or contain
the attack. GRC fails when the control worked and nobody can prove it did.

## The comparison

:::versus a="GRC" a-badge="GOVERNS & EVIDENCES" b="Cyber security" b-badge="BUILDS & DEFENDS"
Decides which controls the organisation is obliged to run, defines what
"working" means, checks that they run and keeps the evidence a customer or
auditor will ask for.
+++
Designs, builds and operates the defences (firewalls, identity, monitoring and
incident response), and answers to whether an attack is stopped, not to whether
the paperwork exists.
:::

:::attrs a="GRC" b="Cyber security"
The question it answers :: Can we prove the control is required, owned and operating? :: Does the defence actually stop the attack?
Primary output :: Evidence, policy, register :: Configured, running defences
Relationship to the control :: Requires and evidences it :: Builds and operates it
Measured on :: Audit and assessment outcomes :: Incidents prevented and contained
Typical background :: Audit, risk, compliance, IT :: Engineering, networks, security operations
Reports to :: Varies: CISO, risk, legal or COO :: Usually the CISO
Fails when :: The control worked but nobody can prove it :: The defence does not stop or contain the attack
:::

The "relationship to the control" row is where the two most often get miscast.
Security operates the control; GRC establishes that it is required, that a named
person owns it and that it ran, while the control itself is usually owned by the
business or system owner, not by either function. Neither role covers the other,
which is why one function cannot simply absorb the work of the other.

## Where they overlap

Both live at the control, and that is the whole source of the confusion. A single
access-review control is designed and run by security, and separately recorded by
GRC as required, owned by a named person and operated on a schedule. That last
part is the evidence. Neither produces the audit outcome alone: a control that
runs but is undocumented fails the audit, and a documented control that does not
run fails the attack.

NIST CSF 2.0 made the split easier to see. Its February 2024 revision added a
sixth function, GOVERN, alongside the five operational functions (Identify,
Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) precisely because organisations were maturing
defences with nobody owning the decisions, roles and oversight behind them.
GOVERN is not a seventh silo, though: it sits across the framework and informs how
the other five are run. It elevates the governance and risk-oversight outcomes a
security-GRC role tends to concentrate on, while the operational functions are
where the defences are built and run, a single framework holding both kinds of
work.

## Where people wrongly merge them

Two mistakes recur. The first is imagining a GRC analyst configures firewalls.
The daily tools of GRC are documents, interviews and evidence requests; the
analyst reads a system diagram and challenges an engineer's answer but does not
touch the console. Hiring a GRC analyst to harden systems, or a security engineer
to run an ISO 27001 audit programme, produces someone doing half of each job
badly.

The second is assuming a strong security team makes GRC redundant: that if the
defences are genuinely good, the governance layer is overhead. It does not
follow. An auditor does not accept "our security team is excellent" as evidence,
and a customer's procurement questionnaire does not accept it either. ISO/IEC
27001 certification assesses whether the ISMS is implemented and effective, which
turns on documented information and objective evidence that the controls operate,
not on the operator's confidence that they do. Excellent security with no
evidence trail struggles to demonstrate an effective ISMS, and cannot be sold to
a buyer who requires certification.

## Where GRC reports

There is no single answer, and the honest one is that the reporting line varies
by organisation. GRC reports to the CISO in many technology companies, where the
work is mostly security compliance and that is where it already sits. It reports
to a Chief Risk Officer where risk is a board-level function in its own right,
common in financial services. It reports to legal or the General Counsel where
the binding obligations are contractual and regulatory more than technical, and
to the COO where GRC is treated as operational assurance spanning more than
security.

What drives the choice is the breadth of the mandate. A function scoped to
information security alone tends to sit under the CISO. One that also covers
privacy, financial reporting, health and safety or supplier conduct cannot sit
cleanly under security, because it would then be governing controls its own boss
operates, the self-review problem under another name.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is GRC cyber security?

No, though in a technology company the two overlap enough to be staffed from one
team. Cyber security is one obligation domain GRC covers, alongside privacy,
financial reporting and whatever else binds the organisation. Security builds and
operates the defence; GRC decides the defence is required, checks it runs and
keeps the proof. Where a company does only security compliance, "GRC" and
"security" can look like the same job. The boundary gets clearer the broader the
mandate is.

### Is GRC a technical role?

Not in the sense of configuring systems, though it is a literate one. You need to
read a network diagram, follow an engineer's explanation and challenge it; you do
not need to build or run the system. The daily work is documents, interviews and
evidence. It varies with seniority and company (a GRC engineer automating
evidence collection sits closer to the console than a compliance analyst chasing
a quarterly access review), but the core skill is judgement about obligations and
proof, not hands on the firewall.

### Does GRC sit under the CISO?

Sometimes, not always. It reports to the CISO where its scope is information
security; to a Chief Risk Officer, legal or the COO where the mandate is broader
than security. A GRC function that governs controls the CISO's own team operates
has an independence problem if it also reports to that CISO, which is one reason
larger organisations move it out from under security as it grows.
:::

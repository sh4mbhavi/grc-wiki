:::lede
The **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** is the European Union's binding data-protection law — Regulation (EU) 2016/679, in force since 25 May 2018 — governing how personal data about people in the EU is handled. It is legislation, not a voluntary framework.
:::

That single fact changes what the rest of this part means. Most other documents in this part are things an organisation elects to adopt; GDPR, like the HIPAA Security Rule, applies whether or not anyone has decided to engage with it, and a regulator can act without a contract or a customer asking it to. It appears among the frameworks because most organisations run their obligations under it as a compliance programme, but the obligation itself is statutory.

## A law, not a framework

Reading GDPR as one more optional standard is the mistake everything else follows from. Its reach also does not stop at the EU border. Article 3 makes the Regulation **extraterritorial**: it binds a controller or processor established outside the EU whenever they offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behaviour. A company with no European office, billing in dollars, is caught the moment it targets or tracks people in the Union.

This is the honest reason GDPR sits in this part and also the reason it does not behave like its neighbours. [PCI DSS](/frameworks/pci-dss/) is a contract with the card brands; ISO 27001 is a certificate you choose to pursue. GDPR is neither — it is law, and the only comparable neighbours here are other statutory regimes such as the [HIPAA Security Rule](/frameworks/hipaa-security-rule/). If you are unsure which kind of document you are holding, [how to read a framework](/frameworks/how-to-read-a-framework/) sorts them.

:::def term="Framework" ref="5.0"
A framework, in the sense used across this part, is a document an organisation chooses to adopt and be measured against. GDPR is not one: it applies by operation of law, with or without any decision to take it on.
:::

## Controller and processor

GDPR splits responsibility between two roles, and the split decides who answers for what. The **controller** decides the purposes and means of processing — the why and the how — and carries the primary legal accountability. A **processor** processes personal data on the controller's behalf and only on its documented instructions, typically a supplier running a service. The distinction is not cosmetic: it determines who owes which duty, and the two must have a written contract under Article 28 governing the processing. A vendor that quietly starts deciding its own purposes for the data has become a controller in its own right, with a controller's liabilities.

## Principles, lawful bases and rights

Article 5 sets out seven principles that every act of processing has to satisfy.

:::terms
Lawfulness, fairness and transparency :: Process on a valid legal basis, do not mislead the data subject and be open about what happens to their data.
Purpose limitation :: Collect data for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and do not reuse it in ways incompatible with those.
Data minimisation :: Hold only the data the purpose actually needs, and no more.
Accuracy :: Keep personal data correct and current, and correct or erase what is wrong.
Storage limitation :: Keep data in identifiable form no longer than the purpose requires.
Integrity and confidentiality :: Secure the data against unauthorised access, loss or damage — the security principle.
Accountability :: Be able to demonstrate compliance with the other six, not merely assert it.
:::

Accountability is the principle that turns the rest into work you can show. The controller must be able to produce the records, assessments and evidence that [demonstrate compliance](/compliance/what-compliance-produces/), because under GDPR being compliant and being able to prove it are the same requirement.

Article 6 then says every act of processing needs a **lawful basis**, and there are six: consent, a contract with the data subject, a legal obligation, protection of someone's vital interests, a public task and legitimate interests. At least one must apply before processing begins, and the basis has to be fixed up front — you cannot reach for a different one afterwards to justify what you have already done. Consent is only one of the six and often the weakest, because it must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and it can be withdrawn at any moment. For routine business processing a contract, a legal obligation or legitimate interests is usually the sounder footing.

GDPR also gives data subjects a set of enforceable **rights** over their own data: the right to be informed; access to it; rectification of inaccurate data; erasure, the "right to be forgotten"; restriction of processing; data portability; objection to processing; and rights concerning solely automated decision-making and profiling. The rights are not absolute — several carry exemptions — but a controller has to be able to recognise a request, respond within the statutory deadline and explain any refusal.

## Key obligations

The principles and rights translate into a handful of concrete duties a supervisory authority will expect to see met.

:::terms
Records of processing activities :: Article 30 — a documented inventory of what personal data you process, why, where it flows and how long you keep it.
Data protection by design and by default :: Article 25 — build data protection into systems and processes from the outset, and default to the least data and the tightest sharing.
Data protection impact assessment (DPIA) :: Article 35 — a structured risk assessment required before high-risk processing, such as large-scale profiling or systematic monitoring.
Breach notification :: Articles 33 and 34 — unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk, notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and generally within 72 hours of becoming aware; under Article 34, tell affected individuals without undue delay where the breach is likely to result in a high risk to them.
Data protection officer (DPO) :: Article 37 — appoint one where you are a public authority (other than a court acting judicially), or where your core activities involve large-scale regular and systematic monitoring or large-scale processing of special-category or criminal-conviction data.
:::

The 72-hour clock is the one that catches people out. It runs from awareness of the breach, not from the end of the investigation, so the notification often goes in while the facts are still incomplete.

## Enforcement, fines and "GDPR certification"

GDPR is enforced by national **supervisory authorities** — the data protection authorities, one or more per member state, such as Ireland's Data Protection Commission or France's CNIL. Where processing crosses borders, a lead authority coordinates through a one-stop-shop mechanism. Their power is real: administrative fines run in two tiers, each set at the higher of a fixed ceiling or a percentage of worldwide turnover.

| Tier | Maximum fine | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lower | EUR 10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher | Breaches of specific obligations such as records, security and DPO duties |
| Higher | EUR 20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher | Breaches of the core principles, lawful-basis rules and data-subject rights |

There is no single certificate that makes an organisation "GDPR compliant". Article 42 provides for approved certification mechanisms that can help demonstrate compliance for specific processing, but no scheme signs off the whole Regulation, and "GDPR certified" presented as a blanket badge is marketing rather than a legal status.

Following the UK's departure from the EU, the **UK GDPR** is the retained domestic version, broadly mirroring the EU text and enforced by the Information Commissioner's Office. An organisation handling both UK and EU data is generally subject to both regimes at once.

## Where it goes wrong

**Treating it as optional.** The commonest error is filing GDPR alongside the voluntary standards and waiting to be asked. It is directly applicable law: the duty exists the moment you process the data, and a supervisory authority needs no contract or complaint to act.

**Assuming distance is protection.** "We are not in the EU, so it does not apply" ignores Article 3. Offer goods or services to people in the EU, or monitor their behaviour, and you are in scope with no European office at all — and may additionally have to appoint an EU representative under Article 27.

**Leaning on consent for everything.** Consent is one lawful basis of six, and often the most fragile, because it can be withdrawn and must clear a high bar to be valid. Processing that would sit comfortably on contract, legal obligation or legitimate interests is left exposed when it is bolted to a consent the data subject can revoke at will.

**Buying a "GDPR certification".** No product or audit makes an organisation compliant with the whole Regulation. Article 42 schemes can evidence compliance for a defined activity, but a vendor selling blanket "GDPR certification" as proof of compliance is overstating what it can deliver, and relying on it leaves the actual obligations unmet.

:::faq
### Does GDPR apply to my company if it is based outside the EU?
It can. Article 3 gives GDPR extraterritorial reach: if you offer goods or services to people in the EU, or monitor their behaviour, you are in scope even with no EU establishment. Where you have no EU presence you may also have to appoint an EU representative under Article 27. Being outside Europe is not, by itself, a reason the Regulation does not reach you.

### Do I always need consent to process personal data?
No. Consent is only one of six lawful bases, and often not the best. For much routine processing a contract, a legal obligation or legitimate interests gives you a stronger footing, and consent means honouring a withdrawal at any time. You need one valid basis, fixed before processing starts — not necessarily consent.

### Can I get my organisation "GDPR certified"?
Not in the sense the phrase implies. There is no official certificate that renders an organisation compliant with the whole Regulation. Article 42 allows approved schemes that can evidence compliance for particular processing, and those can help, but any vendor selling blanket "GDPR certification" as proof of overall compliance is overstating what it means.
:::

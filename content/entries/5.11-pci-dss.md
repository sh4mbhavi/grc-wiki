:::lede
The **Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS)** is a set of security requirements that any organisation storing, processing or transmitting cardholder data must meet. It is maintained by the PCI Security Standards Council and enforced contractually by the card brands, not by any law.
:::

The standard applies equally to merchants and to the service providers that handle card data on their behalf. What varies is not whether it applies but how much of it applies and how that is proven, both of which follow from how card data moves through the business.

## What it is and who enforces it

PCI DSS is published and maintained by the **PCI Security Standards Council (PCI SSC)**, a body founded in 2006 by the five major card brands — Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover and JCB. The Council writes the standard; it does not enforce it.

Enforcement runs through contracts. A merchant's obligation to comply is a term of its agreement with its acquiring bank, and the acquirer in turn is bound by the operating rules of the card brands. **PCI DSS is not a law.** The common belief that "PCI is a legal requirement" is wrong in most jurisdictions: it is a private contractual scheme. That distinction matters because the consequences are contractual too — an acquirer can levy fines, raise transaction fees or ultimately withdraw a merchant's ability to accept cards, and after a breach the brands can pass assessment and recovery costs down the chain. A few jurisdictions reference PCI DSS in statute, but the standard itself is industry rule rather than legislation.

The **cardholder data environment (CDE)** is the people, processes and systems that store, process or transmit account data. That environment, and any connected-to or security-impacting system component that is not effectively isolated from it, falls within PCI DSS scope.

## The six goals and twelve requirements

PCI DSS groups its detailed controls under six goals, which resolve into twelve numbered requirements.

:::terms
Build and maintain a secure network and systems :: Requirements 1 and 2 — network security controls and secure configurations, in place of default passwords and settings.
Protect account data :: Requirements 3 and 4 — protect stored account data and encrypt cardholder data in transit across open, public networks.
Maintain a vulnerability-management programme :: Requirements 5 and 6 — protection against malicious software and secure development, patching and change control.
Implement strong access-control measures :: Requirements 7, 8 and 9 — least-privilege access, unique identification with authentication and restriction of physical access.
Regularly monitor and test networks :: Requirements 10 and 11 — logging and monitoring, plus regular security testing including internal and external vulnerability scans and penetration tests.
Maintain an information security policy :: Requirement 12 — the governing policies, risk assessment and programme that hold the rest together.
:::

Requirements 6 and 11 are the ones a GRC function will already recognise: between them they cover secure software, patching and the scanning-and-testing cycle that a standing [vulnerability management](/compliance/vulnerability-management/) programme produces. The evidence a merchant gathers for those requirements is largely the same evidence [vulnerability management](/compliance/vulnerability-management/) generates on its own account.

## Version 4.0 and the customised approach

**PCI DSS v4.0** was published in 2022; **v4.0.1** (2024) is the current revision. v4.0 ran alongside the retired v3.2.1 through a transition period, and a large block of future-dated v4.0 requirements took effect on **31 March 2025**.

The headline change is a second route to meeting a requirement.

**Defined approach.** Implement the requirement as written and be assessed against that wording. This is the traditional model and stays available for every requirement.

**Customised approach.** Meet the stated security objective of a requirement by controls of your own design, then evidence that they achieve the objective. The assessor validates the design against a controls matrix and a targeted risk analysis rather than against prescriptive text. It suits mature organisations and adds documentation burden; it is not a shortcut.

v4.0 also introduces **targeted risk analyses**, which let an organisation set the frequency of certain recurring activities from documented risk rather than a fixed interval, and it expands **multi-factor authentication**, extending MFA to all access into the CDE rather than only remote or administrative access.

## How compliance is validated

Validation scales with volume. Each card brand sorts merchants into four **merchant levels** by annual transaction count, and Level 1 also captures any merchant that has suffered a breach, whatever its size. Service providers sit in a separate two-level scheme. The thresholds below are the common shape; each brand sets and can vary its own.

| Merchant level | Who it covers | Typical validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Highest volume — commonly more than 6 million transactions of a brand a year, and any merchant after a breach | Annual on-site assessment by a QSA — or, where the brand permits, an Internal Security Assessor (ISA) with the required officer sign-off — producing a Report on Compliance (ROC) and an Attestation of Compliance (AOC); quarterly ASV scan where externally accessible in-scope systems exist |
| Level 2 | Commonly 1–6 million transactions a year | Annual Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ) and AOC; quarterly ASV scan where externally accessible in-scope systems exist; some brands require assessor involvement |
| Level 3 | Commonly 20,000–1 million e-commerce transactions a year | Annual SAQ and AOC; quarterly ASV scan where externally accessible in-scope systems exist |
| Level 4 | Fewer than 20,000 e-commerce, or up to 1 million other transactions a year | Annual SAQ and AOC; quarterly ASV scan where externally accessible in-scope systems exist |

The **SAQ** is a self-assessment, and the type — A, A-EP, B, B-IP, C, C-VT, D or P2PE — depends on how the merchant handles card data: a fully outsourced e-commerce page; a payment page that partly touches your systems; standalone terminals and so on each map to a different questionnaire and a different set of applicable requirements. It is the same idea as any [security questionnaire](/compliance/security-questionnaires/) used as a self-assessment, but the SAQ is PCI-specific and unrelated to the vendor-assessment templates such as the SIG or CAIQ. Where in-scope system components are externally accessible, quarterly scans by an **Approved Scanning Vendor (ASV)** are required (Requirement 11.3.2), subject to the applicable SAQ and validation rules.

## Scope is the biggest lever

The single largest determinant of cost and effort is not the standard's depth but how much of the business the CDE touches. Everything that stores, processes or transmits account data — and everything connected to it without segmentation — is in scope. Shrink that footprint and both the applicable requirements and the SAQ shrink with it.

Three techniques do most of the work. **Tokenisation** replaces the card number with a non-sensitive token so downstream systems never hold real account data. **Point-to-point encryption (P2PE)**, using a validated solution, encrypts the card data at the point of capture so your own systems only ever handle ciphertext. **Outsourcing** the card handling itself — a hosted payment page or a redirect to a compliant service provider — moves the sensitive processing outside your environment entirely.

None of these makes compliance disappear. The belief that "using a PCI-compliant processor makes us compliant" is a common and expensive error: outsourcing narrows your scope, often down to the shortest questionnaire (SAQ A), but you still complete the validation your brand or acquirer requires — an SAQ and AOC when eligible, or a ROC where directed — and still carry the requirements that remain: how you integrate the processor, who has access and how you govern the relationship.

## Where it goes wrong

**Treating PCI as a law.** It is a contract with your acquirer, not a statute. Teams that wait for a regulator to act miss that the enforcement lever — fines, fees, loss of card acceptance — sits with the bank and can move faster than any regulator would.

**Assuming a compliant processor confers compliance.** A compliant service provider reduces your scope; it does not complete your validation. You still submit your own SAQ and AOC for the slice of the flow you own.

**Never segmenting the CDE.** Left flat, a network puts every connected system in scope, which turns a small card footprint into an enterprise-wide assessment. Segmentation reduces the assessed scope and effort, but it does not by itself change a merchant's brand or acquirer validation level — it will not turn a required ROC into an SAQ.

**Reading validation as a point rather than a programme.** A ROC or SAQ describes a moment; v4.0's business-as-usual emphasis exists because controls decay between annual assessments. Compliance evidenced once a year and ignored for the other eleven months is the ordinary way a breached-but-"compliant" merchant is made.

:::faq
### Is PCI DSS a legal requirement?
In most jurisdictions, no. PCI DSS is a contractual standard imposed by the card brands and enforced through your acquiring bank, not a law passed by a legislature. The obligations are real — an acquirer can fine a merchant, raise its fees or withdraw card acceptance, and the brands can pass breach costs down the chain — but they flow from contract and brand operating rules rather than statute.

### What are the PCI DSS merchant levels?
There are four merchant levels, set by each card brand according to annual transaction volume, with Level 1 the highest. Level 1 also captures any merchant that has suffered a breach. Level 1 typically requires an annual on-site assessment by a QSA producing a Report on Compliance and an Attestation of Compliance; Levels 2 to 4 typically validate through a Self-Assessment Questionnaire and Attestation of Compliance, with quarterly ASV scans where card data reaches internet-facing systems.

### Does using a PCI-compliant payment processor make my business compliant?
No — it narrows your scope but does not remove your obligation. Outsourcing card handling to a compliant processor can reduce you to the shortest questionnaire, often SAQ A, but you still complete the validation your brand or acquirer requires for your part of the flow — usually an SAQ and AOC: how you integrate the processor, who can access the environment and how you govern the relationship all remain yours to validate.
:::

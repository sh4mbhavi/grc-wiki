:::lede
Governance, risk and compliance is the coordinated set of practices an
organisation uses to *direct* what it is trying to achieve, *understand* what
could stop it, and *prove* it meets the obligations that apply to it.
:::

The three are administered by different people and fail together. Governance
sets the appetite that risk measures against; compliance supplies most of the
evidence that governance reviews. Run them as three teams with three
spreadsheets and the first audit finds the contradictions for you.

The label is younger than the work. Boards have directed, risk functions have
measured and compliance officers have evidenced for as long as there have been
regulated companies. What changed around 2002 was that the same board committee
started receiving all three reports in the same meeting, and noticed they did
not agree.

## The three pillars

:::terms
Governance :: Who decides, on what authority, and where the decision is recorded — policy set, committee calendar, delegated authority matrix, board reporting.
Risk :: Identifying what could obstruct the objectives, sizing it, deciding what to do, then watching it — register, appetite statement, treatment plan, issue log.
Compliance :: Knowing which obligations bind you — law, contract, standard — mapping each to a control, and keeping the proof that the control ran.
:::

Each pillar owns artefacts, not opinions. A governance function that cannot
produce the minutes recording a decision has not governed it. A risk function
whose register has no owners and no dates is a list. A compliance function
holding a policy but no evidence the policy was followed has documented an
intention.

## How the three connect

:::diagram name="grc-loop" n="1" caption="**The GRC loop.** Break any edge and the function becomes paperwork: appetite nobody set, controls nobody asked for, evidence nobody reads."
:::

Read the loop clockwise and it describes a quarter of work. Governance publishes
an appetite statement — say, no accepted risk above a residual score of 12
without board sign-off. Risk applies that threshold to the register and produces
a treatment plan for everything above it. Compliance runs the controls those
treatments name and keeps the evidence. Assurance reports the whole picture
back, and governance revises the appetite it set.

Read it anticlockwise and it describes how the function fails. Evidence
accumulates with no control mapped to it. Controls run with no risk behind them.
Risks are scored against an appetite that was never written down.

:::def term="Appetite" ref="3.2"
The amount of risk an organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its
objectives, stated by governance and used by risk to decide what must be
treated.
:::

## Where the work actually happens

:::split ratio="1.25fr 1fr"
Most of a GRC analyst's week is spent moving a risk or a control through a small
number of states, and keeping the record of that movement legible to someone who
was not there. The interview, the register update, the evidence request, the
follow-up when the evidence does not arrive.

The matrix is the argument, not the answer. Two people scoring the same incident
will land in adjacent cells and both be defensible; what makes the score usable
is that the scale is written down, applied the same way twice, and challenged in
a forum with minutes. A 5 × 5 grid with no documented criteria behind each band
produces numbers that cannot survive a question.

## Frameworks, in one table

| Framework | Type | Answers the question | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Certification | Is the ISMS working? | Certificate |
| NIST CSF 2.0 | Framework | How mature are we? | Profile |
| SOC 2 | Attestation | What can we show a customer? | Auditor report |
| COSO ERM | Model | How does risk reach the board? | Risk view |

They are not alternatives, and picking between them is usually the wrong
question. ISO 27001 and SOC 2 answer different buyers: a certificate travels,
an attestation report is read. NIST CSF 2.0 is a language for describing where
you are, not a bar to clear — its 2024 revision added a sixth function, *Govern*,
precisely because organisations were maturing controls without anyone owning the
decisions behind them. COSO ERM sits above all of it and is about how risk
reaches a board at all.

Most organisations of any size end up mapping between at least two of them. That
mapping — the crosswalk — is the single most reused artefact in the discipline,
because one control almost always satisfies several obligations at once.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is GRC a technical job?

No, but it is a *literate* one. You need to read a system diagram and challenge
an engineer's answer; you do not need to configure the system. The daily tools
are documents, interviews and evidence.

The failure mode is not being insufficiently technical. It is accepting an
answer you did not understand because asking again felt awkward.

### Is GRC the same as cyber security?

No. Cyber security is one obligation domain among several — GRC also covers
financial reporting, privacy, health and safety, supplier conduct and whatever
else the organisation is bound by.

The overlap is large in technology companies, which is why the two are often
staffed from the same team, and why "GRC" is frequently used as shorthand for
"security compliance". That shorthand is what makes the boundary confusing.
Security engineering builds and operates the control; GRC decides the control is
required, tests that it ran, and keeps the proof.

### Which certification should I start with?

If you want the vocabulary, ISO/IEC 27001 Lead Implementer or the ISACA CRISC
syllabus will both give you it. If you want an employer to recognise the letters,
CISA is the most widely asked-for in audit-adjacent roles and does not require a
technical background.

Certification is not the gate people entering the field assume it is. A candidate
who can walk an interviewer through a risk they assessed end to end — how it was
raised, scored, treated, and closed — outperforms one with three certificates and
no artefact to describe.

### Can I move into GRC from IT support?

It is one of the more common routes in. Service desk work already teaches the two
things the role is built on: reading a system you did not build, and writing down
what happened in a way someone else can act on.

The gap to close is the evidence habit — knowing what makes a screenshot
admissible, why a date matters, and what an auditor will ask for next. That is
learnable in months, and considerably faster than teaching a compliance
specialist how a directory service works.
:::

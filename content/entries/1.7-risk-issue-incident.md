:::lede
A risk is a possible future effect of uncertainty — it has not happened yet. An
issue is a risk that has materialised, or a present problem demanding action
now. An incident is a discrete event that has already occurred, often a control
failure or a security event.
:::

The distinction is one of tense. Risk is future and uncertain, issue is present
and certain, incident has already occurred — though its consequences may still be
unfolding. Where each is logged, who owns it and what happens next all follow
from where the thing sits on that timeline.

Practitioners collapse the three constantly, and the collapse is not cosmetic.
An incident filed as a risk overstates open exposure — it counts a thing that
already happened as a thing that might. A live issue left in the register as a
"risk" hides that action is already overdue. Either way the register stops
describing the world.

## The three, defined

:::terms
Risk :: A possible future effect of uncertainty on objectives — something that has not happened and may not. ISO 31000:2018 defines it as the "effect of uncertainty on objectives", where an effect is a deviation from the expected, in either direction.
Issue :: A risk that has materialised, or a present problem that needs action now. It has no single ISO definition; in common operational and project-management usage it names a condition already affecting delivery that must be managed, as distinct from a risk that only might.
Incident :: A discrete event that has already occurred, typically a control failure or, in security, an unwanted event that harms operations. ISO/IEC 27000 separates an information security *event* — something happened that may matter — from an *incident*, an event or series with significant potential to compromise operations.
:::

## Tense is the whole distinction

| Concept | Tense | Where it is logged | Who acts | Worked example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Risk | Future, uncertain | Risk register | Risk owner | A key supplier could fail and take checkout down |
| Issue | Present, certain | Issue log | Action owner | The supplier has raised prices past budget and a decision is needed this quarter |
| Incident | Past, occurred | Incident log or ticketing | Incident responder | The supplier's outage took checkout down for 90 minutes on 14 August |

The example column runs one thread through all three deliberately. The same
supplier is a risk while the failure is hypothetical, an issue the moment a
present problem needs deciding, and an incident once an event has actually
landed. Nothing about the supplier changed; the tense did.

## Where each is logged, and who owns it

These arrangements are common practice rather than anything a standard mandates.
A risk lives in the risk register and, in most schemes, belongs to a named risk
owner — one person who can authorise its treatment rather than a team (see
[3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/)). An issue lives in an issue log, sometimes
a tab on the same spreadsheet and sometimes a separate action tracker, and
belongs to whoever owns closing it. An incident lives in an incident log or the
ticketing system and belongs to the responder handling it until it is contained
and reviewed.

They are kept as separate records for the same reason the three concepts are
separate (see [1.3](/foundations/four-artefacts/)): a single document that mixes
future exposure with present problems and past events cannot be sorted, counted
or reported without first pulling the three back apart.

## How one becomes another

A risk becomes an issue at the moment it materialises. The uncertain future
effect is now a present fact, and it moves from the register — where it was being
watched — to the issue log, where it gets an action owner and a due date. The
risk line does not simply vanish; it is marked as realised and cross-referenced,
so the history survives the question an auditor asks after the fact. Nor does the
risk always close: recurrence or knock-on uncertainty can keep a line open on the
register even after this instance has landed.

An incident runs the other way. A single event can open several new risks — what
else is exposed the same way — and several issues that must be fixed now, and
closing it well usually means adding or strengthening a control. That control
changes the residual score of the risk the incident sat under, which is the
point at which the incident log, the issue log and the register are meant to
reconcile. When they do not, one of the three is lying.

## Why mixing them corrupts the register

A register that quietly fills with issues and incidents is no longer a risk
register; it is a to-do list with scores on it. Two specific corruptions follow,
and both are common.

An incident logged as a risk overstates open exposure. Risk reporting counts
what is open and above appetite, so a past event sitting in that total is read as
a live threat and the risk picture looks worse than reality — or the incident's
one-off severity inflates a score that then never comes back down.

A live issue left in the register as a "risk" does the opposite and hides that
action is already required. A risk carries a treatment decision and a review
date; an issue carries an owner and a deadline. Dressed as a risk, an overdue
problem reads as something merely being monitored, and the deadline that should
be escalating does not exist.

The tell, when reviewing someone else's register, is a row whose statement is in
the past tense. "Supplier X failed and checkout was down" is not a risk — it
already happened. It belongs in the incident log, with whatever new risk it
exposed rewritten as its own forward-looking line.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is an issue just a realised risk?

Often, but not only. Many issues began as a risk on the register that then
materialised — the supplier you flagged did raise its prices. But an issue can
also be a present problem nobody had logged as a risk: a defect found in
production, a control that turns out never to have run. The test is not where it
came from but its tense — an issue is a problem that exists now and needs a
decision now, whatever its history.

Treating every issue as a "realised risk" also tempts you to reopen the risk
line and re-score it, when what the issue actually needs is an action owner and a
date, not a fresh likelihood.

### What is the difference between an issue and an incident?

An incident is a discrete event that occurred at a point in time — an outage, a
breach, a control failure — and in security ISO/IEC 27000 draws a further line
between an *event* (something happened that might matter) and an *incident* (an
event, or series, likely to harm operations). An issue is broader and need not
be a single event: a supplier that keeps missing SLAs, a project running over
budget or an unresolved control gap are all issues without being incidents.

Put plainly, an incident is something that happened; an issue is something that
is wrong. An incident is closed once it is contained and reviewed; an issue is
closed once the underlying problem is resolved.

### Does every incident create a risk?

No, but a serious one usually should. The value of an incident to the risk
function is what it reveals — an incident that exploited a control gap is
evidence the residual risk was understated, and the post-incident review is
where that score gets corrected and a new treatment added. An incident fully
contained by controls working as designed confirms the existing risk rather than
opening a new one.

The failure mode is the incident that closes with a "lessons learned" slide and
never touches the register, so the same event recurs against a risk line that
still reads green.
:::

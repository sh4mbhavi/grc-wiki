:::lede
Progression in GRC is not doing more of the analyst job faster. Each move up
hands you a different thing to own - first a framework, then a programme, then
the board's picture of risk - and the people who stall are usually the ones
doing the lower job extremely well.
:::

The ladder has three real rungs, whatever the titles in between suggest. What
separates them is not time served but what you can be handed without supervision.

## The three moves

**Analyst to manager.** The analyst runs the register, chases evidence and
completes questionnaires - work measured on whether the artefacts are current and
legible. The manager owns a framework end to end and the audit relationship that
comes with it. The step is the first time you carry a right to say no: to a
control owner, to a timeline, to a scope. Nobody hands that over because you
processed questionnaires quickly; they hand it over once you have shown judgement
about which risks matter and which findings are worth the fight.

**Manager to head.** The manager answers for a framework; the head answers for
the programme and the narrative the board hears. The work shifts from controls to
resourcing, from the audit to the story of the function's risk position. This is
the move where technical fluency stops being the constraint and political
literacy starts - the head who cannot get a budget conversation to land is
limited regardless of how well they understand the controls.

**The sideways move.** More common than either, and not a demotion. Risk to
privacy, compliance to internal audit, GRC to security architecture or into
product. Each rebuilds depth in a new domain on top of the general grounding, and
a person with two domains behind them is worth more than one with a single deep
specialisation, particularly on the route into consulting.

## What earns each move

Not certifications, and not tenure. The signal is the same at every rung: an
artefact you owned and a decision you made that turned out to be right.

The analyst earns the manager seat by showing judgement - pushing back on a
finding, spotting a risk before it was raised, running a piece of the framework
without being told the next step. The manager earns the head seat by owning an
outcome the board saw: an audit that produced no surprises, a risk position that
matched reality when it was tested. Each move is granted on evidence of the next
job, done early, not on mastery of the current one.

## Where people plateau

The permanent analyst is the common one: someone excellent at the mechanics who
never develops the judgement calls that the next seat is made of. The mechanics
are visible and rewarded, so it is comfortable to stay, and the register keeps
running. The way out is to start making the calls before you are asked to - form
a view on which risks matter, argue a finding, own a decision - because the seat
is granted to people already doing its work, not promised to people who have
earned a turn.

The second plateau is the specialist who went deep too early, built a career on
one framework, and finds the head-of-function role wants breadth they traded away.
Depth is valuable and it is also a ceiling if it is the only thing you have.

## Common questions

:::faq
### How long does it take to go from analyst to manager?

There is no fixed number, because the move is granted on demonstrated judgement
rather than years served. Some make it inside two years by owning real decisions
early; others run the register competently for longer without ever showing the
judgement the next seat requires. Time is a weak predictor; what you have owned is
a strong one.

### Do I need a certification to get promoted in GRC?

Rarely for the move itself. Certifications help you get hired and pass CV
screens; promotion turns on whether you have shown you can do the next job. A
certification that matches your target seat - CISM heading into management, CRISC
into a risk role - can support the case, but it does not make it. See
[6.3](/career/certifications-and-training/).

### Is GRC manager a technical role?

Usually it shifts away from hands-on work rather than toward it: the job is owning
a framework, the audit relationship and a set of decisions, and the constraint is
judgement more than technical depth. How much fluency it needs still varies by
organisation and domain - a manager over cloud or product controls reads more
architecture than one over policy and third-party risk. You need enough to
challenge an engineer's answer either way.
:::

:::lede
The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework** is a voluntary, outcome-based structure for organising and communicating a cyber risk-management programme, built around six functions - govern, identify, protect, detect, respond and recover - rather than a catalogue of controls to implement.
:::

Version 2.0, published in 2024, made two changes worth knowing before anything else. It widened the stated audience from critical-infrastructure operators to organisations of every size and sector, and it added GOVERN as a sixth function that wraps the other five. The framework certifies nothing: its value is a common structure for a programme and a shared vocabulary for reporting cyber risk to leadership, and through GOVERN it ties that risk to [enterprise governance](/governance/what-governance-produces/) (2.0).

## The six functions

The Core organises everything the framework covers into six functions. Five are operational; GOVERN sits around them and sets the terms they operate under.

:::terms
Govern :: Sets and monitors the organisation's cybersecurity strategy, expectations and policy. Added in version 2.0 and positioned around the other five.
Identify :: Establishes a current understanding of assets, suppliers and the risks that bear on them.
Protect :: Puts safeguards in place to keep those risks from being realised.
Detect :: Finds and analyses possible attacks and compromises.
Respond :: Acts on a detected incident to contain and mitigate it.
Recover :: Restores the assets and operations an incident has affected.
:::

## Outcomes, not a control catalogue

The Core is a three-level hierarchy - functions divide into categories, and categories into subcategories - and the subcategories are outcomes, not controls. A subcategory states a result to achieve, such as "identities and credentials are managed", and says nothing about the mechanism that achieves it.

The mechanism lives elsewhere. NIST and third parties publish Informative References - non-exhaustive mappings from the Core outcomes to control sets such as NIST SP 800-53, ISO/IEC 27001 and the CIS Controls - so the framework points at controls rather than listing them. Treating a subcategory as a control to tick off, rather than selecting controls to achieve it, misreads what the document is. Mapping those references onto your own control inventory is a [crosswalk](/frameworks/building-a-crosswalk/) (5.2), and the framework is built to sit at one end of one.

## Tiers describe rigour, not maturity

The framework defines four tiers describing how rigorous an organisation's cyber risk governance and management are. They are not maturity levels, and tier 4 is not a target every organisation should reach - you choose the tier that fits the risk you carry and the resources you have. The construct they are most often confused with is [control maturity](/frameworks/control-maturity/) (5.9), which rates how well a given control is embedded rather than how an organisation governs risk overall.

| Tier | Name | Character of risk governance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Partial | Risk managed ad hoc and reactively, with little organisation-wide awareness. |
| 2 | Risk Informed | Risk practices approved by management but not established organisation-wide. |
| 3 | Repeatable | Practices set formally as policy and updated as risk and business change. |
| 4 | Adaptive | Practices adapt continuously from lessons learnt and predictive indicators. |

:::def term="CSF Tiers" ref="5.9"
Four descriptions - partial, risk informed, repeatable and adaptive - of the rigour of an organisation's cyber risk governance. Deliberately not maturity levels, and no single tier is the right answer for everyone; a small firm may sit at tier 2 by design.
:::

## Profiles turn the framework into a plan

A profile records how the functions, categories and subcategories apply to a particular organisation. Two profiles do the real work: a Current Profile describing the outcomes you achieve today, and a Target Profile describing the outcomes you intend to achieve. The gap between them is the input to an action plan - what to close, in what order and at what cost.

These are Organisational Profiles, specific to one organisation. A Community Profile is a shared baseline built for a sector or a common use case - a starting Target Profile that organisations facing the same risk can adopt rather than draft from scratch.

## Where it goes wrong

**Read as a maturity ladder.** Teams treat the tiers as levels to climb and set tier 4 as the goal. The tiers describe how rigorous risk governance is, not how advanced an organisation is, and choosing a lower tier deliberately is a legitimate outcome of a risk decision.

**Mistaken for a control catalogue.** Because subcategories read like requirements, they get implemented directly and the Informative References go unread. The subcategory names the outcome; the control that delivers it is chosen separately - often via the Informative References into 800-53, ISO/IEC 27001 or the CIS Controls - and skipping that choice leaves a programme with outcomes stated but no controls behind them.

**A Current Profile with no target.** A profile of today, drawn up and never paired with a Target Profile, produces a description and no plan. The gap between current and target is the whole mechanism; on its own a Current Profile documents posture but sets no direction.

**Chasing a certificate.** Procurement asks for "CSF certification" and a supplier offers one. Neither exists. What exists is a self-assessment against the framework, or a third-party assessment of it, and calling either a certification misrepresents what has been done.

## Common questions

:::faq
### Is the NIST Cybersecurity Framework mandatory?
No. It is voluntary. It was written first for critical-infrastructure operators and, from version 2.0, for organisations of any size and sector, but it imposes no obligation on the private sector by itself. A regulator, a contract or a board can require a particular organisation to use it, in which case the requirement comes from that instrument and not from the framework.

### Can you get NIST CSF certified?
No. There is no CSF certification and no certificate to hold up. You can assess yourself against the framework, or have a third party assess you, and you can record your Current and Target Profiles, but none of that is an accredited certification in the sense that ISO/IEC 27001 is. A supplier claiming to be "CSF certified" is describing an assessment, not a certificate.

### What changed in NIST CSF 2.0?
Two things stand out. GOVERN was added as a sixth function, wrapping the other five and tying cyber risk to the organisation's wider risk management and governance. And the scope widened from critical infrastructure to organisations of every size and sector. The 2024 revision also expanded the supporting resources, including the online Reference Tool that publishes the Informative References.
:::

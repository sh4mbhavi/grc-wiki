:::lede
**Control maturity** is a rating of how consistently, how well documented and how repeatably a control or process is performed — a measure of the process behind a control, not of whether that control works on any given day. It is scored on a scale, most often the five levels adapted from the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI), and it is not an assurance opinion.
:::

The score is useful for one thing: deciding where to invest to make a control more reliable. It is misused for almost everything else, usually by being read as a statement that the control works.

## The scale

Most maturity models in governance, risk and compliance use a five-level scale derived from CMMI. Each level describes how the process behind the control is run, from improvised to continuously improved. Some models add a level 0 for a control that does not exist at all.

| Level | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Non-existent | Added by some models. The control is not performed and there is nothing to assess. |
| 1 | Initial | The work gets done, but the process is unpredictable, reactive and dependent on individuals rather than a repeatable method. |
| 2 | Managed | Planned, performed, monitored and controlled at the level of individual projects, with policies and records — but not yet standardised across the organisation. This is the level older CMM-based models called "repeatable". |
| 3 | Defined | Documented as an organisation-wide standard and performed consistently, whoever runs it. |
| 4 | Quantitatively managed | Controlled using statistical and other quantitative techniques against defined objectives, so process performance is understood in numbers rather than merely tracked. |
| 5 | Optimising | Continuously improved using that measurement; change is deliberate rather than reactive. |

A score is only as good as the rubric behind it. Level 3 has to mean the same evidence for two different assessors, or the number cannot be rolled up or compared over time.

## Maturity is not effectiveness, and not coverage

This is the point the whole entry turns on. Three different questions are routinely collapsed into one number, and they measure different things.

| Dimension | The question it answers | What a good score still hides |
|---|---|---|
| Maturity | How repeatable and well documented is the process behind the control? | Whether the control actually stops anything. |
| Effectiveness | Did the control achieve its objective across the period? | Whether it will survive staff turnover or scale. |
| Coverage | How much of the estate does the control reach? | Whether it works where it does reach. |

A high-maturity control can still fail on a given day — the process is repeatable, but the run in question missed. A low-maturity, ad hoc control can still catch the risk once, by luck or by an individual's diligence. Maturity describes the machine, not the output on any particular day, and it yields no assurance on its own.

:::def term="Operating effectiveness" ref="4.2"
Whether a control actually achieved its objective across a period of operation — the question an auditor tests, and the source of assurance. Distinct from maturity, which describes only how the process is run.
:::

## Capability and maturity

CMMI offers two representations, and the difference matters when someone reports a level. The **continuous** representation gives selected process areas their own capability levels — useful when you want to know how one area stands. The **staged** representation gives an organisational unit a single maturity level once it satisfies a required set of process areas — one benchmark for the whole.

A capability level describes one area; a maturity level describes the organisation. A claim like "we are level 4" means nothing until it says level 4 of what — one control, one domain or the organisation as a whole.

## Using maturity in a programme

Maturity earns its place as a planning tool, not a scorecard. Three disciplines make it work.

**Set a target by risk, not a target of level 5.** Maturity costs money to build and money to hold. Decide, per control or per domain, what maturity the risk it carries actually justifies. A low-risk control held at level 5 is spending that a higher-risk control still at level 2 needed. Not everything needs to be optimised.

**Track trajectory, not just position.** For prioritisation the direction over time often tells you more than the absolute level. A control moving from 2 to 3 is being invested in; one static at 4 for three years may be coasting.

**Prioritise by the gap.** The distance between current and target maturity, weighted by the risk behind each control, is a defensible way to sequence investment. The controls with the widest risk-weighted gaps go first.

Maturity levels are sometimes confused with **NIST CSF Tiers**, which are a related but distinct governance construct. The [NIST Cybersecurity Framework](/frameworks/nist-csf-2/) defines four Tiers — Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable and Adaptive — describing the rigour of an organisation's cybersecurity risk governance rather than the maturity of an individual control. NIST is explicit that the Tiers are not maturity levels and that Tier 4 is not a target every organisation should set; the right Tier is the one that meets the organisation's goals and reduces risk to an acceptable level. Read a maturity model the same way, and read it against [how to read a framework](/frameworks/how-to-read-a-framework/) before you adopt one.

## Where it fails

**Rated on opinion, with no rubric.** Without defined evidence for each level, a maturity score is a feeling written as a number. Two assessors score the same control two levels apart, the roll-up is noise and the year-on-year comparison measures nothing but who did the assessing.

**Reported as an assurance opinion.** A maturity score says the process is repeatable and documented; it says nothing about whether the control operated effectively over the period. Presenting "level 4" to a board as though it were assurance overstates what was measured. Assurance comes from effectiveness testing, not from a maturity rating.

**Level 5 set as the target for everything.** Driving every control to Optimising diverts budget and attention from controls that carry real risk and are still at level 2. Uniform targets are the most common way a maturity programme wastes money.

**High maturity mistaken for "we are secure".** A control can be documented, measured and continuously improved and still be the wrong control, or aimed at a risk that no longer matters. A mature process around an obsolete control is a well-run irrelevance. Maturity of a process is not evidence of a defended organisation.

:::faq
### What is the difference between control maturity and control effectiveness?
Maturity measures the process — how consistently, how well documented and how repeatably a control is performed. Effectiveness measures the outcome — whether the control achieved its objective across a period. A control can score high on maturity and still fail on a given day, and a barely-documented control can still catch a risk once. A maturity score is not an assurance opinion; effectiveness testing is where assurance comes from.

### What are the five levels of a maturity model?
Most models use the five levels adapted from CMMI: 1 Initial (unpredictable and reactive), 2 Managed (planned and controlled per project, not yet standardised), 3 Defined (standardised across the organisation), 4 Quantitatively managed (controlled with quantitative techniques) and 5 Optimising (continuously improved). Some models add a level 0, Non-existent, for a control that is not performed at all.

### Should every control reach maturity level 5?
No. Target maturity should be set by the risk each control carries, not raised uniformly. Holding a low-risk control at level 5 costs money to build and maintain that a higher-risk control still at level 2 needed more. The goal is the right maturity for each control and a closing gap over time, not level 5 everywhere.
:::

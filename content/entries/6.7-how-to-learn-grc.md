:::lede
Learning GRC from no starting point is a sequence, not a syllabus: get the
vocabulary, understand the four artefacts the function produces, build one of
them yourself, choose the seat you are aiming at, and only then pick a
certification. The step people skip is the third one, and it is the step that
gets the interview.
:::

There is no degree requirement and no single mandatory course. What an employer
is checking for is whether you can read a system you did not build, form a
defensible judgement about a risk, and write it down so someone else can act on
it. Those are learnable in months, and most of the learning is in doing the work
once rather than reading about it many times.

## The path

:::terms
1 — Vocabulary :: Learn what governance, risk and compliance each mean, how they connect, and the terms that recur — control, evidence, appetite, residual risk, assurance, three lines. Start at [1.1](/foundations/what-grc-is/) and [1.2](/foundations/terms-and-definitions/). Budget a week or two of reading.
2 — The four artefacts :: Understand the risk register, the policy set, the control-to-obligation mapping and the evidence file — what each is, who owns it, what makes it defensible. [1.3](/foundations/four-artefacts/) is the anchor. This is the mental model everything else hangs on.
3 — Build one artefact :: Pick a small real-ish scope — a side project, a club, a fictional company — and produce an actual risk assessment or a control set with evidence. This is where understanding becomes skill, and it is the thing you will walk an interviewer through. [6.1](/career/breaking-into-grc/) describes the walk-through.
4 — Learn one framework properly :: Read ISO/IEC 27001 or the NIST CSF 2.0 end to end and map your artefact to it. One framework known well beats five skimmed. [5.14](/frameworks/iso-27001-explained/) and [5.5](/frameworks/nist-csf-2/) are the starting points.
5 — Pick a target seat :: Audit, risk, compliance, security-focused GRC. The seat decides which certification is worth money and which vocabulary to deepen. [6.0](/career/grc-roles/) lays out the options.
6 — Choose a certification :: Now, with a seat in view, the matching credential is worth pursuing — CISA for audit, CRISC for risk, CISM for security management. Before a seat is in view, a certification is vocabulary with nothing to attach it to. See [6.3](/career/certifications-and-training/).
:::

## How to run the learning itself

Two viable routes, and a supplement that helps either.

- **Self-structured.** Use the primary sources — NIST, CIS, ISO previews,
  regulator guidance — and a reference like this one to build your own sequence.
  Free, and effective if you are disciplined about finishing step 3 with a real
  output. The failure mode is reading indefinitely and never building anything.
- **A taught course.** A dedicated GRC course supplies the sequence, the pacing
  and, in the better ones, the artefacts. [6.6](/career/grc-courses-and-training/)
  compares the types; [GRC Mastery](https://grcmastery.com) is one built for this
  exact from-scratch audience, covering the whole function and having you produce
  the artefacts as you go.
- **Either way**, join where practitioners talk — forums, communities, local
  chapters — and get one person to critique your artefact before you put it in
  front of an interviewer.

## A realistic timeline

Studying part-time alongside a job, most people reach interview-ready — vocabulary
solid, one framework known, one artefact built and rehearsed — in **three to six
months**. Faster is possible full-time; much faster usually means step 3 was
skipped. Certification adds time on top of that and is better sequenced after the
first role or once a specific seat is confirmed.

## Where self-teaching goes wrong

**Collecting courses instead of finishing one.** Three half-watched
video series and no artefact. Depth in one, finished, beats breadth across five.

**Certification first.** A CISA study guide bought in month one, memorised
without context, and mostly forgotten by the time there is a job to apply it to.

**No output.** Months of reading, nothing built, so the interview question "walk
me through a risk you have assessed" has no answer.

**One framework's worth of tunnel vision.** Learning only the vocabulary of a
single employer's framework and being lost when the next one uses different
words for the same ideas. Learn one well, then read a second to see the pattern.

## Common questions

:::faq
### How do I get into GRC with no experience?

Learn the vocabulary and the four core artefacts, then build one of those
artefacts yourself against a small real scope — a risk assessment or a control
set with evidence. Map it to one framework you have read properly. That artefact,
which you can walk an interviewer through end to end, is what substitutes for
experience at the entry level. Adjacent backgrounds like IT support, audit or
administration transfer well.

### How long does it take to learn GRC?

Studying part-time, roughly three to six months to be ready for entry-level
interviews: the vocabulary, one framework known well, and one artefact you have
built and can discuss. Becoming competent in a seat takes a year or more of doing
the work; certifications sit on top of that timeline, not before it.

### Can I learn GRC on my own?

Yes. Every concept is in freely available primary sources — NIST, CIS, ISO
previews, regulator guidance — and references like this one. Self-teaching works
if you impose structure and finish with a built artefact. Many people use a
taught course instead precisely because the structure and the artefact are the
hard parts to supply yourself.

### Do I need a degree to work in GRC?

No. There is no degree requirement for most GRC roles. Employers look for the
ability to understand a system, judge a risk and document it clearly. A degree in
any field helps at the screening stage; a demonstrable artefact and a clear grasp
of the vocabulary matter more in the interview.

### Should I get a certification before applying for GRC jobs?

Usually only if you already know the specific seat you are targeting, in which
case the matching credential (CISA, CRISC, CISM) helps pass the CV screen. If you
do not have a seat in view, a broad grounding plus a built artefact is a better
use of the same time, because a specialist certification learned without context
does not stick and does not close an interview on its own.
:::

:::lede
A committee's terms of reference set out its mandate, membership and decision
rights - what it exists to decide, who sits on it and what it must escalate.
:::

The terms of reference are what separate a committee from a recurring meeting. A
meeting gathers people; a committee holds a mandate, and the terms of reference
are where that mandate is written down and bounded. Take them away and the group
has no way to show that a decision it made was one it was entitled to make.

## What the terms of reference contain

Seven things, and a set of terms of reference that is missing one of them tends
to fail at exactly that point.

:::terms
Purpose and mandate :: The class of decision the committee owns - the reason it exists and the matter it may settle. If you cannot name that class, you have scheduled a meeting.
Membership and chair :: Who sits on it, who chairs it and who may send a deputy. Seats are named by role, not by person, so the committee survives someone leaving.
Quorum :: The minimum attendance for a decision to stand. Set low enough that the forum can actually meet, and specific enough that three people from the same team cannot constitute it.
Frequency and cadence :: How often it meets, and the out-of-cycle path for a matter that cannot wait for the next scheduled sitting.
Decision rights and delegated authority :: What the committee may settle alone, up to what limit, and what it must send higher. See [2.2](/governance/committees-and-delegated-authority/) for the matrix that records this.
Reporting and escalation line :: Who the committee reports to, what it must escalate and on what trigger. See [2.3](/governance/board-reporting/) for what that report looks like when the parent body is the board.
Review cycle :: When the terms of reference are themselves re-read and re-approved - commonly once a year - and by whom.
:::

No single standard dictates what these must contain. ISO 37000 frames how a
governing body sets a mandate and delegates authority to the bodies beneath it,
and IIA guidance sets out what an audit committee's charter should establish; but
the specific conventions above - a stated quorum, an annual review - are
governance good practice rather than a clause you can be marked against.

## Why an auditor asks for it first

A committee with no current terms of reference makes decisions it cannot evidence
the authority for. The first thing an auditor or an external reviewer asks a
committee is not what it decided but by what right: show me your terms of
reference, and show me you met quorum when you decided it. A decision minuted by
a forum that cannot produce a current mandate, or that was taken below quorum, is
a decision the organisation may have to unpick.

:::def term="Quorum" ref="2.2"
The minimum attendance for a committee's decisions to stand. A decision taken
below it is invalid on the committee's own terms, whatever its merits.
:::

This is why the terms of reference are read together with the minutes, never on
their own. The document states the rule; the minutes are where you find out
whether the committee kept it.

## Where terms of reference fail

Three failure modes recur, and each one is visible the moment you read the
minutes against the terms.

The stale mandate is the first. The terms were written when the committee was formed and
never reviewed since. The remit has drifted - the committee now decides things
the document does not mention, or has stopped covering things it lists - and
nobody has reconciled the two. The review cycle exists to catch this; a committee
whose terms carry no review date usually has not.

The quorum that is never met is the second. The terms set a quorum the committee cannot
realistically achieve, so meetings proceed anyway with whoever turned up. Every
decision taken below quorum is, on the committee's own terms, technically
invalid, and a reviewer who counts attendees against the stated quorum will find
them. A quorum nobody meets is worse than a low one, because it makes valid
decisions look defective and defective ones look valid.

Deciding outside the mandate is the third. The committee routinely settles matters that
belong to another forum or to a higher authority, because it is the meeting that
happens to be in the room. This is the failure the delegated authority matrix
exists to prevent, and it surfaces when two committees are found to have decided
the same thing differently.

## Common questions

:::faq
### What is the difference between terms of reference and a charter?

Mostly regional vocabulary for the same document. "Charter" is the more common
word in North American practice and for audit committees specifically; "terms of
reference" is standard in UK, Commonwealth and much European governance. Both
name the document that sets a committee's mandate, membership and authority.

Where a distinction is drawn, a charter is sometimes treated as the broader
founding document approved by the board, with the terms of reference as the
operating detail beneath it. The usage is not consistent, so read whichever word
your organisation uses as meaning the mandate document.

### Who approves a committee's terms of reference?

The body that created the committee and delegated authority to it - usually the
board for a board committee, or the parent committee for a sub-committee. A
committee does not approve its own terms of reference, for the same reason a
function does not sign off its own audit: the authority to set the mandate has to
come from above the mandate. In practice the committee drafts them and the parent
body approves them.

### How often should terms of reference be reviewed?

Annually is the common convention, and many committees take their terms as the
first item at a set meeting each year. There is no mandated interval - review
cadence is governance good practice rather than a rule you can be measured
against - but the review should also be triggered by change: a new regulator, a
merger, a shift in remit. The failure to guard against is terms reviewed on paper
without being read, so the review quietly re-approves a drift nobody examined.
:::

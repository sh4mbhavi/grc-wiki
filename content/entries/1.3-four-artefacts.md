:::lede
Strip a GRC programme of its tooling, its committee names and its reporting
templates, and four records remain. Everything else in the function exists to
create, maintain or read one of them.
:::

The four are a policy set, a risk register, a control inventory and an evidence
store. A programme missing one is not necessarily failing, but it will be unable
to answer a specific class of question - and the question always arrives.

## The policy set

The written answer to *what are we required to do, and who said so*. A policy
states the rule; a standard states the measurable version of the rule; a
procedure states the steps. Three levels, and the confusion between them is the
most common documentation problem in a young programme.

The test of a policy set is not whether it exists. It is whether an employee who
breaks a rule can be shown the sentence they broke, and whether the person who
approved that sentence can be named.

## The risk register

The list of things that could obstruct the objectives, with a score, an owner
and a decision attached to each. See [3.1](/risk/reading-a-risk-register/) for
how to read one.

A register goes stale in a predictable way: entries stop changing. If nothing in
a register has moved a band in two quarters, either the environment is
extraordinarily stable or nobody is reviewing it. In practice it is the second.

## The control inventory

The list of things you actually do to keep those risks inside appetite, each one
mapped to the obligations it satisfies. This is the artefact that makes multiple
frameworks affordable - one access review can satisfy an ISO 27001 Annex A
control, a SOC 2 criterion and an internal policy at the same time, but only if
something records that it does.

An inventory with no mapping column is a to-do list. The mapping is the artefact.

## The evidence store

The proof that the controls ran. Screenshots, exports, tickets, signed
attestations, meeting minutes - with dates, and with a name attached to whoever
produced them.

The quality bar is not "we have a folder". It is that a stranger can pick a
control, pull the evidence for a specific month, and tell from the evidence alone
that the control operated. If the evidence needs a colleague to explain it, an
auditor will treat it as absent.

:::def term="Evidence" ref="4.2"
A dated record produced by the operation of a control, sufficient for a
reviewer who was not present to conclude the control ran as described.
:::

## How they fail together

The four are not independent. A control inventory with no risk register behind it
produces controls nobody can justify the cost of. A risk register with no policy
set behind it produces scores nobody can challenge, because there is no stated
threshold to challenge them against. An evidence store with no inventory is a
warehouse.

The order to build them in, when starting from nothing, is register first, then
policy, then inventory, then evidence. Starting with policy is the intuitive
choice and the slow one: you end up writing rules for risks you have not yet
established you carry.
